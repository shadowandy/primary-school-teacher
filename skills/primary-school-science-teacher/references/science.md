# Science — Primary 3 to 6

Source: *Science Teaching and Learning Syllabus, Primary Three to Six, Standard / Foundation* — MOE Curriculum Planning and Development Division, ©2022, implementation starting with the 2023 Primary Three cohort, updated Jan 2026. The syllabus PDF is **not shipped with this skill**: everything the skill has about Science is in this file and in [`science-answering-techniques.md`](science-answering-techniques.md). The bracketed page numbers record provenance for anyone auditing this file against the source document — they are not a place you can go and read. Where this file is silent on something a user needs, say it is not in the source rather than filling the gap from general science knowledge; invented MOE detail reads as correct and a parent cannot catch it.

Science begins at **P3**. At P5-P6 it splits into **Standard** and **Foundation**; P3 and P4 have one track. Content is organised as five themes, not as per-level units: **Diversity, Cycles, Systems, Interactions, Energy**. A theme reappears at several levels with a different topic each time, so always pair the theme with the level. The themes are not sealed off from each other — the syllabus says there are no clear boundaries between them and some topics are common to more than one, so a P6 question on seed dispersal can sit under Interactions (adaptation) or Cycles (reproduction) [p12].

Each topic's learning outcomes come in three columns: **Core Ideas** (the content), **Practices** (what the student does — investigate, compare, classify), and **Values, Ethics and Attitudes**. All three are examinable territory; Practices is where open-ended and experiment-design questions come from.

**Open-ended questions are marked on the chain**, not on the final claim: the concept that is operating, then every link from the condition in the question to the outcome observed. Read [`science-answering-techniques.md`](science-answering-techniques.md) when tutoring, setting or marking an *explain* or *why* question, writing a model answer or mark scheme for one, or diagnosing a child who knows the science and still loses marks. It is a router: it carries the five moves every answer makes, the boundary notes and the pitch, and sends you to the frames for one topic at a time and to the misconceptions to name when marking. It is an account of how open-ended answers are marked in the classroom, not quoted syllabus; this file stays the authority on content and on every boundary below.

So: **Energy conversion is the one topic a Foundation child does not meet at all.** Every other P5-P6 topic has its own Foundation outcome set, with fewer core ideas and lower-demand verbs. The syllabus marks Foundation exclusions by underlining topics in its overview table, and underlining does not survive text extraction — the statement above is read off the per-topic learning-outcome pages instead, where Energy Conversion is the only topic with no Foundation page [p44-81].

That is the whole subject. A topic a user names that is not in these two tables is not in this syllabus — say so rather than placing it.

## Standard vs Foundation: read the verb

The split is mostly in the command verb, and it sets how much a question may demand:

- Foundation uses **state, recognise, identify** — recall and simple identification.
- Standard uses **show an understanding of, describe, differentiate, relate** — explanation and application.

So at P5 water, Standard asks the child to *show an understanding of how water changes from one state to another*; Foundation asks them to *state* the changes [p50, p52]. A Foundation paper that demands a causal explanation is above-level.

The verb shifts in the Practices column too, and it shifts what an experiment question may ask: Standard *investigates*, Foundation *observes and compares*. At P5 plant transport, Standard investigates how food and water are transported; Foundation observes it [p58, p59].

The glossary at the end of this file defines every command word. Use those definitions when setting and marking — they are MOE's own.

# Boundaries

The syllabus's **"not required"** notes are the most useful content in the whole document, because they are where a well-meaning adult most reliably goes above-level. These are hard limits for setting, marking and explaining. They are reproduced close to verbatim below, attached to the level and stream whose outcome page carries them.

## P3

**Diversity of living and non-living things** [p39] — Recall of names of specific living things (e.g., guppy) and their characteristics (e.g., give birth to young alive) is not required.

**Diversity of materials** [p40-41] — The use of terms *transparent / translucent / opaque* is not required; the "transparency" of a material refers to whether the material allows most / some or no light to pass through. The concept of density is not required — the "ability to float on water" refers to whether an object is able to stay on or near the surface of water and not sink. The focus is on how the properties of materials are used. Definitions in use: the "strength" of a material is its ability to be subjected to loads without breaking; the "flexibility" of a material is its ability to bend without breaking; a material is "waterproof" when it does not absorb water.

**Life cycles** [p43] — The focus is on the stages (seed, young plant, adult plant) of flowering plants. Processes in plant reproduction (pollination, fertilisation, seed dispersal and germination) are introduced in the topic of Cycles in Plants and Animals (Reproduction) in P5. Animal life cycles are limited to chicken, cockroach, frog, grasshopper, beetle, butterfly, mosquito.

**Magnets** [p63] — Recall of magnetic materials such as nickel and cobalt is not required. Magnetic shielding and magnetic induction are not required.

## P4

**Human system (digestive)** [p54] — The human-systems learning outcome introduces students to an overview of human systems in the body. Detailed knowledge of the muscular and skeletal systems (such as names of the bones/muscles in the body and descriptions of how they work) are not required. The digestive system itself is limited to mouth, gullet, stomach, small intestine and large intestine.

**Plant system (plant parts and functions)** [p57] — Food-carrying and water-carrying tubes in the plant transport system are introduced in the topic of Plant System (Respiratory and Circulatory Systems) in P5. The parts here are leaf, stem, root.

**Light** [p76] — The law of reflection is not required. The use of specific terms *transparent, translucent, opaque* is not required.

**Heat** [p78] — Recall of the rate of heat transfer of specific materials (such as different types of metals) is not required. Good conductors: metals. Poor conductors: wood, plastics, air, rubber.

## P5

**Reproduction — Standard** [p44-46] — Vegetative propagation methods, such as stem cutting, are not required. The use of specific terms ("self-pollination" and "cross-pollination") to describe the pollination process is not required. Knowledge of the pollen tube formation is not required. The specific location where fertilisation takes place in the female reproductive system is not required. Foetal development and the mechanism of obtaining air, food and water through the umbilical cord are not required. What is required as definition: fertilisation occurs when a male reproductive cell fuses with a female reproductive cell; in humans, fertilisation occurs when a sperm fuses with an egg; the fertilised egg develops in the womb; students should know that ovaries produce eggs, and the testes produce sperms.

**Reproduction — Foundation** [p47-48] — Vegetative propagation methods, such as stem cutting, are not required. The use of specific terms ("self-pollination" and "cross-pollination") to describe the pollination process is not required. Foetal development and the mechanism of obtaining air, food and water through the umbilical cord are not required. What is required as definition: fertilisation is the process that leads to the development of fruits and seeds; in humans, fertilisation occurs when a sperm fuses with an egg; the fertilised egg develops in the womb; students should know that ovaries produce eggs, and the testes produce sperms. The Foundation page does not carry the pollen-tube note or the location-of-fertilisation note, because neither idea is reached at all — the plant processes are only *stated*.

**Human respiratory and circulatory systems — Standard and Foundation alike** [p55-56] — Detailed knowledge of respiratory system (e.g., alveoli) and circulatory system (e.g., heart chambers and valves) is not required. The use of specific terms (*xylem, phloem, stomata, artery, vein, capillary*) is not required.

**Plant system (respiratory and circulatory systems) — Standard and Foundation alike** [p58-59] — Recall of the relative positions of water-carrying and food-carrying tubes is not required. The use of specific terms (*xylem* and *phloem*) is not required. The concept of transpiration pull is not required.

## P6

**Interaction of forces — Standard and Foundation alike** [p65, p67] — Direction of frictional force for "rolling objects" such as wheels and balls is not required. The use of specific terms such as *air resistance* and *water resistance* is not required. At Foundation, *elastic spring force* is additionally out: it is neither in the topic title nor in the list of types of force [p66].

**Energy conversion (Standard only)** [p81] — The use of specific terms (*chemical potential energy, gravitational potential energy* and *elastic potential energy*) is not required; the forms required are kinetic, potential, light, electrical, sound and heat energy.

**Photosynthesis — Standard** [p79] — The focus of respiration is on the release of energy from food. At Foundation, respiration is not an outcome at all [p80].

**Interactions within the environment — adaptations, Standard and Foundation alike** [p70, p73] — Students are introduced to the types of dispersal methods and physical characteristics of different fruits and seeds in the theme of Cycles. The focus in this theme is to help students recognise that physical characteristics are the "structural adaptations" which help fruits and seeds in their dispersal.

## Foundation outcomes that simply are not there

These are not quoted "not required" notes — they are ideas and investigations that appear in a Standard outcome list and have no counterpart on the matching Foundation page. They bind just as hard when setting a Foundation paper, so they are listed separately rather than dressed up as syllabus quotations.

- **P5 Water** [p50-52] — Foundation has no *importance of water to life processes* and no *impact of water pollution on Earth's water resources*. Its only Practice is *compare water in 3 states*: the investigation of heat gain/loss on temperature and state, and the investigation of the factors affecting the rate of evaporation (wind, temperature, exposed surface area), are Standard Practices. A Foundation question on what makes washing dry faster has no outcome behind it.
- **P5 Reproduction** [p44-48] — Foundation has no *cell is a basic unit of life*, no *living things reproduce to ensure continuity of their kind and many characteristics are passed on from parents to offspring*, and no *recognise the similarity in terms of fertilisation in flowering plants and humans*.
- **P5 Human respiratory and circulatory systems** [p55-56] — Foundation has no *integration of the digestive, respiratory and circulatory systems in carrying out life processes*. Its comparison Practice is *how plants and humans take in oxygen and give out carbon dioxide* — fish are not in it, and the comparison of how substances are transported within plants and humans is Standard only.
- **P5 Electrical system** [p60-61] — Foundation investigates number of bulbs arranged **in series only**; bulbs in parallel are Standard.
- **P6 Photosynthesis** [p79-80] — Foundation has only *the Sun is our primary source of energy (light and heat)*. Respiration and *differentiate between the ways in which plants and animals obtain energy* are Standard.
- **P6 Interactions within the environment** [p68-74] — Foundation has no *effect on organisms when the environment becomes unfavourable*, no **food web** (food chain only), and no *differentiate among organism, population and community*. Where Standard says different habitats support different *communities*, Foundation says different *organisms*.

# Content by topic

## Diversity

*Essential takeaways:* there is a great variety of living and non-living things around us; we classify them based on their similarities and differences; maintaining diversity is important for survival. *Key inquiry questions:* what can we observe around us? How can we classify the great variety of living and non-living things? Why is it important to maintain diversity? [p13, p38]

**Diversity of living and non-living things (P3)** [p39] — Describe the characteristics of living things: need water, food and air to survive; grow, respond and reproduce. Recognise some broad groups of living things based on similarities and differences: plants (flowering, non-flowering); animals (amphibians, birds, fish, insects, mammals, reptiles); fungi (mould, mushroom, yeast); bacteria. *Practices:* observe a variety of living and non-living things and infer differences between them; classify living things into broad groups (in plants and animals) based on similarities and differences of common observable characteristics. *Attitudes:* curiosity in questioning and exploring the surrounding living and non-living things; care and concern in being responsible towards living things.

**Diversity of materials (P3)** [p40-41] — Relate the use of various types of materials (wood, metal, ceramic, rubber, glass, plastic, fabric) to their physical properties. *Practices:* compare physical properties of materials — strength, flexibility, ability to float/sink in water, waterproof, transparency. The focus is on how the properties are used, so a question is about choosing a material for a job, not about reciting a property table. *Attitudes:* objectivity in using data and information to validate observations and explanations about the properties and uses of materials.

## Cycles

*Essential takeaways:* there are repeated patterns of change around us; understanding cycles helps us make predictions about events and processes. *Key inquiry questions:* what makes a cycle? How does a cycle help us predict events and processes? Why are cycles important to life? [p14, p42]

**Cycles in plants and animals — life cycles (P3)** [p43] — Show an understanding that different living things have different life cycles, in plants and in animals. *Practices:* observe and compare the life cycles of plants grown from seeds over a period of time; observe and compare the life cycles of animals over a period of time (chicken, cockroach, frog, grasshopper, beetle, butterfly, mosquito). *Attitudes:* curiosity in questioning and exploring the surrounding plants and animals; care and concern in being responsible towards plants and animals.

**Cycles in matter and water — matter (P4)** [p49] — State that matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. Differentiate among the three states of matter (solid, liquid, gas) in terms of shape and volume. *Practices:* measure mass and volume using appropriate apparatus. *Attitudes:* curiosity in exploring matter in the surroundings and questioning what they find.

**Cycles in plants and animals — reproduction (P5)** [p44-48] —
*Standard:* recognise that a cell is a basic unit of life; show an understanding that living things reproduce to ensure continuity of their kind and that many characteristics of an organism are passed on from parents to offspring; describe processes in the sexual reproduction of flowering plants — pollination, fertilisation (seed production), seed dispersal, germination; recognise the process of fertilisation in the sexual reproduction of humans; recognise the similarity in terms of fertilisation in the sexual reproduction of flowering plants and humans. *Practices:* investigate the ways in which plants reproduce — spores, seeds.
*Foundation:* state the processes in the sexual reproduction of flowering plants (the same four); state the process of fertilisation in the sexual reproduction of humans. *Practices:* observe and compare the ways in which plants reproduce — spores, seeds.
*Attitudes (both):* curiosity in questioning and exploring the surrounding plants and animals; care and concern in being responsible towards plants and animals.

**Cycles in matter and water — water (P5)** [p50-52] —
*Standard:* recognise that water can exist in three interchangeable states of matter; show an understanding of how water changes from one state to another — melting (solid to liquid), freezing (liquid to solid), boiling/evaporation (liquid to gas), condensation (gas to liquid); show an understanding of the terms melting point of ice (or freezing point of water) and boiling point of water; show an understanding of the roles of evaporation and condensation in the water cycle; recognise the importance of the water cycle; recognise the importance of water to life processes; describe the impact of water pollution on Earth's water resources. *Practices:* compare water in 3 states; investigate the effect of heat gain or loss on the temperature and state of water — when ice is heated it melts and changes to water at 0 °C, when water is cooled it freezes and changes to ice at 0 °C, when water is heated it boils and changes to steam at 100 °C, when steam is cooled it condenses to water; investigate the factors which affect the rate of evaporation — wind, temperature, exposed surface area.
*Foundation:* recognise that water can exist in three interchangeable states of matter; state how water changes from one state to another (the same four changes); state the melting point of ice (or freezing point of water) and boiling point of water; recognise the changes in states of water in the water cycle; recognise the importance of the water cycle. *Practices:* compare water in 3 states.
*Attitudes (both):* concern for water as a limited natural resource and responsibility in conserving it.

## Systems

*Essential takeaways:* a system is made of different parts, each with its own unique function; different parts of a system influence and work together to perform function(s). *Key inquiry questions:* what is a system? How do different parts/systems work together to perform function(s)? Why is it important to understand how parts/systems work together? [p14, p53]

**Human system — digestive (P4)** [p54] — Identify the human systems in the body and state their functions (digestive, respiratory, circulatory, skeletal and muscular). Identify the parts in the human digestive system (mouth, gullet, stomach, small intestine and large intestine) and describe their functions. *Attitudes:* curiosity in questioning about the structures or functions of the body. This topic has no Practices column entries — an experiment-design question has nothing to stand on here.

**Plant system — parts and functions (P4)** [p57] — Identify the different parts of plants and state their functions: leaf, stem, root. *Practices:* observe plant parts. *Attitudes:* curiosity in exploring the surrounding plants and questioning what they find; care and concern in being responsible towards plants.

**Human system — respiratory and circulatory (P5)** [p55-56] — Both streams: recognise that air is made up of gases such as nitrogen, carbon dioxide, oxygen and water vapour; identify the parts of the human respiratory (nose, windpipe, lungs) and circulatory systems (heart, blood, blood vessels) and **describe** (Standard) or **state** (Foundation) their functions.
*Standard also:* recognise the integration of the different systems (digestive, respiratory and circulatory) in carrying out life processes. *Practices:* compare how plants, fish and humans take in oxygen and give out carbon dioxide; compare the ways in which substances are transported within plants and humans — plants: tubes that transport food and water; humans: blood vessels that transport digested food, oxygen and carbon dioxide.
*Foundation Practices:* compare how plants and humans take in oxygen and give out carbon dioxide.
*Attitudes (both):* objectivity in seeking data and information to validate observations and explanations about the human body.

**Plant system — respiratory and circulatory (P5)** [p58-59] — *Standard:* identify the parts of the plant transport system and describe their functions. *Practices:* investigate how food and water are transported in the plant. *Foundation:* recognise how water is transported from the roots to other parts of the plant and how food is transported from the leaves to other parts of the plant. *Practices:* observe how food and water are transported in the plant. *Attitudes (both):* objectivity in seeking data and information to validate observations and explanations about plant parts and functions; care and concern in being responsible towards plants.

**Electrical system (P5)** [p60-61] — Both streams: recognise that an electric circuit consisting of an energy source (battery) and other circuit components (wire, bulb, switch) forms an electrical system; identify electrical conductors and insulators. Standard: **show an understanding** that a closed circuit allows current to flow; Foundation: **state** that a closed circuit allows current to flow. *Practices (both):* construct simple circuits from circuit diagrams; investigate the effect of some variables on the current in a circuit — number of batteries (arranged in series), and number of bulbs arranged **in series and parallel** (Standard) or **in series** only (Foundation). *Attitudes (both):* concern for the need to conserve and to have proper use and handling of electricity.

## Interactions

*Essential takeaways:* there are interactions among us, living and non-living things in the environment; interactions within the environment can have positive or negative impacts; conservation is important to ensure continuity of life and availability of resources. *Key inquiry questions:* what are the types of interactions around us? How do interactions affect the environment and us? Why is it important for us to conserve the environment? [p15, p62]

**Interaction of forces — magnets (P3)** [p63] — Recognise that a magnet can exert a push or a pull. Identify the characteristics of magnets: magnets can be made of iron or steel; magnets have two poles, and a freely suspended bar magnet comes to rest pointing in a North-South direction; unlike poles attract and like poles repel; magnets attract magnetic materials. Recognise uses of magnets in everyday objects. *Practices:* compare magnets, non-magnetic materials and magnetic materials; make a magnet by the stroke method and the electrical method. *Attitudes:* curiosity in exploring the uses of magnets in everyday life.

**Interaction of forces — friction, gravity, elastic spring (P6)** [p64-67] — Both streams: identify a force as a push or a pull; **show an understanding of** (Standard) or **state** (Foundation) the effects of a force — a force can move a stationary object; a force can speed up, slow down or change the direction of motion; a force can stop a moving object; a force may change the shape of an object; recognise and give examples of the different types of forces — magnetic, gravitational, elastic spring (Standard only), frictional; recognise that objects have weight because of the gravitational force acting on the object. *Practices:* investigate the effect of frictional force on the motion of objects (both); investigate the effects of elastic spring force (Standard only). *Attitudes (both):* objectivity in using data and information to validate observations and explanations about forces.

**Interactions within the environment (P6)** [p68-74] — Both streams: identify the factors that affect the survival of an organism — physical characteristics of the environment (temperature, light, water), availability of food, types of other organisms present (producers, consumers, decomposers); recognise that adaptations serve to enhance survival and can be structural or behavioural — to cope with physical factors, obtain food, escape predators, reproduce by finding and attracting mates or dispersing seeds/fruits; give examples of man's impact (both positive and negative) on the environment — positive impact [conservation, reforestation], negative impact [depleting natural resources, deforestation, pollution (land/water/air), global warming].
*Standard also:* show an understanding of the effect on organisms when the environment becomes unfavourable (organisms adapt and survive; move to other places or die); show an understanding of the energy pathway from the Sun through living things and identify the roles of various organisms (producers, consumers, predators, prey) in a food chain **and a food web**; differentiate among organism, population and community — an organism is a living thing, a population is a group of organisms of the same kind living and reproducing at a given place and time, a community consists of many populations living together in a particular place; show an understanding that different habitats support different **communities** (garden, field, pond, seashore, tree, mangrove swamp).
*Foundation instead:* recognise the energy pathway from the Sun through living things and identify the roles of various organisms (producers, consumers, predators, prey) in a **food chain**; recognise that different habitats support different **organisms** (the same six habitats).
*Practices (both):* observe, collect and record information regarding the interacting factors within an environment. *Attitudes (both):* care and concern for Man's impact on the environment by being respectful and responsible towards the environment.

## Energy

*Essential takeaways:* energy is required for things to work; there are various forms of energy and they can be converted from one form to another; some sources of energy can be depleted and we play an important role in energy conservation. *Key inquiry questions:* what are the different forms of energy around us? How is energy used in everyday life? Why is it important to conserve energy? [p15, p75]

**Energy forms and uses — light (P4)** [p76] — Recognise that an object can be seen when it reflects light or when it is a source of light. Recognise that light travels in straight lines and thus a shadow is formed when light is completely or partially blocked by an object. *Practices:* investigate the variables that affect shadows formed — shape, size and position of object(s); distance between light source and object, and between object and screen. *Attitudes:* objectivity in using data and information to validate observations and explanations about light.

**Energy forms and uses — heat (P4)** [p77-78] — Identify some common sources of heat. State that the temperature of an object is a measurement of its degree of hotness. State that heat is a form of energy. Differentiate between heat and temperature. Show an understanding that heat flows from a hotter to a colder object/region/place until both reach the same temperature. Relate the change in temperature of an object to the gain or loss of heat by the object. List some effects of heat gain/loss in our everyday life: contraction / expansion of objects (solid, liquid and gas); change in state of matter. Identify good and poor conductors of heat — good conductors: metals; poor conductors: wood, plastics, air, rubber. *Practices:* measure temperature using a thermometer and a datalogger with temperature/heat sensors. *Attitudes:* objectivity in seeking data and information to validate observations and explanations about heat.

**Energy forms and uses — photosynthesis (P6)** [p79-80] — *Standard:* recognise that living things need energy from respiration to carry out life processes; recognise that the Sun is our primary source of energy (light and heat); differentiate between the ways in which plants and animals obtain energy. *Foundation:* recognise that the Sun is our primary source of energy (light and heat). *Practices (both):* investigate the requirements (water, light energy and carbon dioxide) for photosynthesis (production of sugar and oxygen). *Attitudes (both):* objectivity in using data and information to validate observations and explanations about photosynthesis.

**Energy conversion (P6 Standard only)** [p81] — Recognise that energy from most of our energy resources is derived in some ways from the Sun. Recognise and give examples of the various forms of energy: kinetic energy, potential energy, light energy, electrical energy, sound energy, heat energy. *Practices:* investigate energy conversion from one form to another. *Attitudes:* care and concern by being responsible in conserving energy in our everyday life.

# Practices of Science

Three components, all examinable through the Practices column [p7-8]:

- **Ways of Thinking and Doing (WOTD)** — posing questions and defining problems; designing investigations; conducting investigations and testing solutions; analysing and interpreting data; evaluating and reasoning; communicating, evaluating and defending ideas with evidence; making informed decisions and taking responsible actions; using and developing models; constructing explanations and designing solutions.
- **Nature of Scientific Knowledge (NOS)** — science is an evidence-based, model-building enterprise to understand the real world; it assumes natural causes, order and consistency in natural systems; scientific knowledge is generated through established procedures and critical debate; scientific knowledge is reliable, durable, open to change in light of new evidence.
- **Science, Technology, Society and Environment (STSE)** — there are risks and benefits associated with the applications of Science in society; applications of Science often have ethical, social, economic and environmental implications; application of new scientific discoveries often drives technological advancement while advances in technology enable scientists to make new or deeper inquiry.

## Skill progression — P4 against P6

The WOTD table states the progression, and it is a pitch boundary in its own right: the same skill is asked for differently at lower and upper primary [p16-19].

| Skill | By the end of P4 | By the end of P6 |
|---|---|---|
| Designing investigations | **Recognise** a fair test (changed/unchanged variables) | **Design** a fair test (changed/unchanged variables) |
| Conducting investigations | Use senses, apparatus and equipment to gather data; investigate to find out answers to questions (**guided** investigations); record and/or compare observations/data **with suggested scaffolding** | Use **and select appropriate** apparatus and equipment; investigate (**guided and open** investigations); record and/or compare using a variety of forms, e.g. notes, drawings, charts |
| Analysing and interpreting data | **Simple** analysis of data and information in representations (tables, bar and line graphs, charts, diagrams) to infer patterns and relationships or explain findings | Analysis of data and information in the same representations, to the same ends — the word *simple* is what drops |

Posing questions, communicating, making informed decisions, using models and constructing explanations are stated once for both bands: ask questions out of curiosity or to deepen understanding and ask questions which can be investigated; communicate clear explanation and reasoning and seek clarification; state or select options based on appropriate criteria with reasons; use multiple representations to explain concepts and describe and predict phenomena; construct possible explanations and generate ideas.

So at P3-P4, give the child the setup and ask whether it is a fair test; from P5, the child may be asked to design it. For any experiment-design question, expect the child to identify the variable changed, the variable measured, and the variables kept the same — this is what "fair test" means at primary level.

## Values, Ethics and Attitudes

The syllabus advocates eight attitudes, and names the relevant ones topic by topic in the third column [p20]:

- **Curiosity** — desiring to explore the environment and question what is found.
- **Creativity** — seeking innovative and relevant ways to solve problems.
- **Integrity** — handling and communicating data and information with honesty.
- **Objectivity** — seeking data and information to validate observations and explanations without bias.
- **Open-mindedness** — accepting all knowledge as tentative and suspending judgement; tolerance for ambiguity; willingness to change views if the evidence is convincing.
- **Resilience** — not giving up on the pursuit for answers/solutions; willingness to take risks and embrace failure as part of the learning process.
- **Responsibility** — showing care and concern for living things and awareness of our responsibility for the quality of the environment.
- **Healthy scepticism** — questioning the observations, methods, processes and data, as well as trying to review one's own ideas.

## Assessment

School-based assessment is weighted **70%-85% written tests and 15%-30% performance-based assessments**, the same band for Standard Science and Foundation Science [p36]. Performance-based modes the syllabus names: checklists, debates, drama / show and tell, games and quizzes, learning trails, model-making, posters, practicals, projects, reflections / journals, teacher observations; portfolios are also named [p35]. The syllabus does not specify a paper structure, section weighting or question count for Science — if a user asks for the official PSLE or school paper format, say that is not in the syllabus document and work from the paper they have.

# Glossary of command words

MOE's own definitions [p83-84]. Mark to these.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| analyse | to identify the parts of objects, information or processes, and the patterns and relationships between these parts |
| classify | to group objects or events based on common characteristics |
| communicate | to transmit and receive information which is presented in various forms — written, verbal, pictorial, tabular or graphical |
