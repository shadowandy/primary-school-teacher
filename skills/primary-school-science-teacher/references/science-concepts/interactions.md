## Magnets — P3

**What a magnet is**
- A magnet exerts a **push** (repulsion) or a **pull** (attraction).
- Magnets **can be made of iron or steel**. Iron and steel are the **magnetic materials** to teach. (*Nickel and cobalt* — recall not required at P3; do not set or expect them.)
- **Magnetic materials** are attracted by a magnet (iron nail, steel paper clip). **Non-magnetic materials** are not (wood, plastic, glass, rubber, fabric, cardboard, copper, aluminium).

**Shapes** — magnets come in different shapes and sizes, and every one has a North and a South pole: bar, rod, horseshoe, U-shaped, ring, button. On a ring or button magnet the two poles are the two flat faces.

**Properties**

| Property | What it means |
|---|---|
| Strongest at the poles | A bar magnet dipped in paper clips picks up most at the two ends, few or none in the middle |
| Points North-South | A **freely suspended** bar magnet (hung from a thread, free to turn) comes to rest with its N pole pointing North |
| Unlike poles attract | N facing S → **magnetic force of attraction**, the magnets move together |
| Like poles repel | N facing N, or S facing S → **magnetic force of repulsion**, the magnets move apart |
| Attracts magnetic materials | Either pole attracts an iron nail; a nail never repels |
| Broken magnet | Each piece is a complete magnet with its own N and S pole |

- **Repulsion is the only sure test for a magnet.** An iron bar is attracted to both poles of a magnet, so attraction cannot tell a magnet from a magnetic material; only a magnet can be repelled.
- Classifying three bars: one that repels a known magnet is a magnet; one that is attracted to both poles and repels nothing is a magnetic material; one that is neither attracted nor repelled is a non-magnetic material.

**Making a magnet** (both methods are P3 Practices)
- **Stroke method** — stroke a magnetic material (iron nail, steel bar) many times with **one pole** of a magnet, **in one direction only**, lifting the magnet clear at the end of each stroke. The end of the nail where the stroke finishes takes the pole **opposite** to the stroking pole (stroke with N, the finishing end becomes S).
- **Electrical method** — coil insulated wire many times around an iron nail and connect the wire ends to a battery. Current flowing in the coil turns the nail into an **electromagnet**; it loses its magnetism when the current stops. The core must be a magnetic material — see the electromagnet frame in `energy-forces-and-electricity.md`.

**Uses in everyday objects** — refrigerator doors, compasses, magnetic strips on bank cards, mobile phones, laptops and televisions, magnetic-levitation trains.

**Common confusions**
- "All metals are magnetic" — aluminium and copper are not.
- Attraction proves something is a magnet — it does not; see the repulsion test.

## Forces: what a force does — P6 (Standard and Foundation)

A **force** is a **push or a pull**. Effects of a force (Standard: *show an understanding of*; Foundation: *state*):

| Effect | Example |
|---|---|
| Moves a stationary object | Pushing a toy truck so it starts moving |
| Speeds up a moving object | Kicking a rolling ball forward |
| Slows down a moving object | Dragging a foot along the ground to slow a ball |
| Stops a moving object | Trapping a ball under the foot |
| Changes the direction of motion | A racket hitting a shuttlecock back |
| Changes the shape of an object | Hammering a can flat; squeezing a sponge |

**Types of force** at P6: **magnetic, gravitational, frictional** (both streams) and **elastic spring force** (**Standard only** — at Foundation it is neither in the topic title nor in the list of forces).

## Gravitational force — P6 (Standard and Foundation)

- **Gravitational force (gravity)** is the force that **pulls objects towards the Earth**. It acts without contact — a dropped ball falls.
- Objects **have weight because of the gravitational force acting on them**. Weight is the pull of gravity on the object.
- The greater the mass of an object, the greater the gravitational force on it, so the greater its weight.

| | Mass | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | The amount of matter in an object | The gravitational force acting on the object |
| Changes with place? | No — the same on Earth and on the Moon | Yes — less on the Moon, where the pull is weaker |
| Measured with | A balance; grams (g), kilograms (kg) | A spring balance |

The Earth-Moon comparison, and newtons as the unit of weight, sit beyond the P6 outcome — see [Beyond the current syllabus](#beyond-the-current-syllabus).

## Frictional force — P6 (Standard and Foundation)

**Definition** — **friction** is a force that **opposes motion** between **two surfaces in contact**: a block pulled to the left along the ground has friction acting on it to the right. It is a contact force, so name **both** surfaces in an answer (*between the soles of the shoes and the floor*).

**Facts**
- Friction **slows down** and **stops** moving objects.
- The amount of friction depends on the **types of surfaces in contact**: the **rougher** the surfaces, the **more friction**.
- Friction **produces heat** (rubbing hands together) and **wears surfaces down** (worn shoe soles, smooth tyres).
- Moving through air or water also meets friction. (*Air resistance*, *water resistance* — terms not required at P6; say *friction between the parachute and the air*, *friction between the fish and the water*.)

**Investigation (Practice, both streams)** — how surface type affects friction:
- Set-up: the same wooden block pulled by a spring balance across different surfaces (smooth board, sandpaper, carpet).
- **Changed**: type of surface. **Measured**: the reading on the spring balance needed to start the block moving (or the distance a toy car rolls before stopping). **Kept the same**: the block, its mass, the force or slope used to start it, the starting position.
- Result: the rougher surface needs a larger pull / stops the car in a shorter distance → more friction.
- Foundation pitches this as *observe and compare*; reading the spring balance is classroom practice, the outcome itself just names *investigate the effect of frictional force on the motion of objects*.

**Advantages and disadvantages**

| Friction is useful | Friction is a problem |
|---|---|
| Grip on objects — holding a cup, writing with a pencil, gripping a bar | Wear and tear — shoe soles, tyres, machine parts |
| Prevents slipping, sliding and skidding — shoe treads, tyre treads | Slows motion we want — pushing a heavy crate |
| Slows down or stops motion — bicycle brakes | Unwanted heat — overheating in engines |
| Produces heat to keep us warm — rubbing hands | |

**Reducing friction** — lubricating oil or grease on the moving parts of machines and bicycle chains; rollers or wheels under heavy objects; ball bearings between moving parts. Do not ask for the direction of friction on rolling wheels or balls — not required.

**Increasing friction — applications** (each answer: rougher surface → more friction between *A* and *B* → less risk of slipping → safer)
- **Rough strips on stair edges** — rougher than the steps, so more friction between shoe soles and the stair; they wear smooth over time and must be replaced.
- **Chalk on gymnasts' and climbers' hands** — absorbs moisture, increasing friction between the hands and the bar or rock, for a better grip.
- **Textured ramp for wheelchairs** — rougher, so more friction between the wheels and the ramp; the wheelchair is less likely to slide off.
- **Snow chains on tyres** — more friction between the tyres and the icy road, so the car is less likely to skid.

## Elastic spring force — P6 Standard only

- A material that **changes shape or length** when a force is applied and **returns to its original shape** when the force is removed is **elastic** (spring, rubber band).
- A spring is **stretched** by a pulling force and **compressed** by a pushing force.
- The force a stretched or compressed spring exerts is the **elastic spring force**. A spring at its original length exerts none.
- The more a spring is stretched, the larger the force exerted on it. A **spring balance** uses this to measure weight: the heavier the object hung on it, the more the spring extends.

**Investigation (Practice, Standard only)** — the effect of load on a spring:
- Set-up: a spring hung from a clamp, original length measured; loads added one at a time and the length measured each time.
- Worked data: original length 4 cm; with 50 g → 5 cm, 100 g → 6 cm, 200 g → 8 cm.

| Load (g) | 0 | 50 | 100 | 200 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Length (cm) | 4 | 5 | 6 | 8 |
| Extension (cm) | 0 | 1 | 2 | 4 |

- **Extension = stretched length − original length.** A graph of **length** against load is a straight line starting at **4 cm** on the y-axis; a graph of **extension** against load is a straight line through **0**. Children mix the two up — check which one the axis says.
- Here each 50 g adds 1 cm, so the greater the weight, the greater the extension.

For an energy answer (catapult, toy car pushed by a spring) use the energy frame: the stretched or compressed spring stores **potential energy**, converted to kinetic energy. (*Elastic potential energy* — term not required; say *potential energy*.)

## Magnetic force as a type of force — P6 (Standard and Foundation)

- Magnetic forces are pushes or pulls exerted by magnets, and they **act at a distance** — no contact needed.
- **Like poles repel** (magnetic force of repulsion); **unlike poles attract** (magnetic force of attraction); a magnet **attracts** a magnetic material such as an iron rod.
- Frame: *the magnet exerts a magnetic force of attraction on the magnetic material* — not "the magnet pulls it".

## The environment and survival — P6 (Standard and Foundation)

The **environment** of an organism is all the **living and non-living things** that affect its survival. The syllabus factors are the **physical characteristics** of the environment (**temperature, light, water**), **availability of food**, and **other organisms present** (producers, consumers, decomposers).

**Non-living (physical) factors**

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Temperature** | Most organisms die if it is too hot or too cold for them — a durian tree that grows well in Singapore would die in a much colder climate. Temperature also affects how much water is available |
| **Water** | Needed for life processes such as photosynthesis; carries dissolved gases, food and waste through the body; frogs and toads need water to complete their life cycles |
| **Light** | Plants need light energy for photosynthesis; animals use light to find food and avoid danger; some animals (woodlice, earthworms) avoid light and live in cooler, darker places |
| Air | Organisms need oxygen for respiration; plants need carbon dioxide for photosynthesis; aquatic organisms take in air dissolved in water and die if the water lacks it |
| Soil | Different plants grow well in different soils (rice in clayey soil, cactus in sandy soil); plants take in water and mineral salts from soil; earthworms and ants shelter in it from heat, light and predators |

Air and soil are commonly taught alongside the three; the syllabus names temperature, light and water.

**Living factors**
- **Availability of food** — plants make their own food; every other organism depends on plant food directly or indirectly, so the death of plants affects them all. Food gives the energy for life processes.
- **Disease-causing organisms** — diseases are caused by some bacteria, viruses and fungi; an outbreak can wipe out a population, which is why diseased plants are removed and diseased animals isolated.
- **Predators and competitors** — organisms in a habitat are **interdependent**; too many predators, or a rival population competing for the same food, reduces a population.

## Unfavourable environments — P6 Standard only

When a habitat changes so that survival becomes difficult, the environment is **unfavourable**. Organisms then **adapt and survive, move to other places, or die**.

| Event | Effects on organisms |
|---|---|
| **Flood** (heavy rain, or sea levels rising with global warming) | Soil erosion, so plants cannot anchor their roots; plants uprooted and animals drowned; floodwater spreads waterborne disease |
| **Fire** | Surroundings become hot and dry; most plants destroyed; smoke and gases pollute the air; animals have breathing difficulties |
| **A new organism introduced** | A new predator may reduce native populations; it may carry disease |
| **Drought** (a long period with little or no rain) | Aquatic organisms and others that depend on water die |

Foundation has no outcome on unfavourable environments — do not set it there.

## Populations and communities — P6 (definitions Standard only)

Standard *differentiates* organism, population and community; Foundation does not.

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Organism** | A single living thing | One frog |
| **Population** | A group of organisms of the **same kind** living and reproducing in a particular place at a given time | All the guppies in a pond |
| **Community** | All the **different populations** living together in one habitat | Every population in the pond — plants, fish, snails, insects, frogs |

- **Population size** counts every individual of that kind — **adults and young**, larvae and pupae included.
- A population **increases** when more are born than die, or more move in. It **decreases** when more die than are born, when organisms move away, or when disease strikes.

**Interdependence** — populations in a habitat depend on one another:

| Animals depend on plants for | Plants depend on animals for |
|---|---|
| Food | Pollination of flowers |
| Oxygen released in photosynthesis | Dispersal of seeds |
| Shelter and protection | Carbon dioxide released in respiration |
| A place to lay eggs and hide young from predators | Animal waste, which enriches the soil with nutrients |

**Habitats** — different habitats support different **communities** (Standard) / different **organisms** (Foundation). The syllabus six: **garden, field, pond, seashore, tree, mangrove swamp**; these notes carry no organism list for the mangrove swamp. Typical residents of the others:

| Habitat | Conditions | Organisms commonly shown |
|---|---|---|
| Garden or field | Open, sunny | Grass, fern, flowering plants, earthworm, ant, grasshopper, bee, beetle, millipede, spider, snail |
| Pond | Fresh water | Water lotus, duckweed, arrowhead, cabomba, cattail, water snail, tadpole, frog, guppy, dragonfly |
| Tree | Above ground, shaded below | Fern and orchid growing on it, bird, squirrel, bat, lizard, snail, slug, ant |
| Seashore | Salt water, sand and rock | Coconut tree, crab, clam, starfish, barnacle, seagull |
| Rotting log, leaf litter (classroom extras) | Dark, damp, warm, little air movement | Fungi, termite, millipede, centipede, woodlouse, earthworm, slug, snail, spider, skink |

## Food chains and food webs — P6 (food web Standard only)

**Roles** (syllabus roles in **bold**)
- **Producer** — makes its own food (green plants). A producer depends on no other organism for food.
- **Consumer** — cannot make its own food; feeds on plants or other organisms.
- **Prey** — an animal hunted and eaten by another.
- **Predator** — an animal that hunts and eats other animals. A plant-eater is not a predator.
- Herbivore (plants only), carnivore (animals only), omnivore (both) — classroom labels.
- **Decomposer** (fungi, bacteria) — breaks down dead organisms and waste into simpler substances such as mineral salts, water and carbon dioxide (**decomposition**). Decomposers stop the Earth being covered with dead matter and return nutrients to the soil for plants.

**Food chain**
- Shows **who feeds on whom**. Always starts with a **plant or plant part**. Arrows point **from the food to the eater** — read the arrow as *is eaten by*. Write *feeds on* for a plant-eater and *preys on* for an animal-eater, not "eats".
- Example: carrot → rabbit → fox → lion. Decomposers break down all of them and return nutrients to the carrot's soil.
- **Energy pathway**: the **Sun** → producer → consumers. Plants use light energy to make food, and the energy is **stored in the food** they make. (*Chemical potential energy* — term not required; say *energy stored in food*.) When a consumer feeds, it gets some of that energy. All the energy in a food chain comes from the Sun.
- Not all the energy passes on: an organism uses much of its food to release energy for its own life processes.

**Food web (Standard only)** — interconnected food chains in one community; it shows food relationships, predator-prey relationships, and energy passing from producers to consumers.

Worked example — grain is eaten by mouse, grasshopper and chicken; grasshopper by chicken and owl; mouse and chicken by owl. Four food chains:
1. grain → mouse → owl
2. grain → grasshopper → owl
3. grain → grasshopper → chicken → owl
4. grain → chicken → owl

Count chains by starting at each arrow out of the producer and following every branch to an organism nothing eats.

**Predator-prey cycle**

predators feed on prey → prey population falls → less food for predators → predator population falls → less hunting → prey population rises → more food for predators → predator population rises → and round again.

Predators keep prey numbers in check, and predator numbers depend on prey numbers.

**Adding or removing an organism** — populations are interdependent, so the balance shifts. Remove one and its prey may rise while its predators lose food. A new organism may prey on existing ones (reducing them) or be food for them (increasing their food supply). Always trace the effect along every arrow from the changed organism.

## Man's impact on the environment — P6 (Standard and Foundation)

The syllabus asks for examples of both kinds: **positive** — conservation, reforestation; **negative** — depleting natural resources, deforestation, pollution (land, water, air), global warming. These notes cover the negative impacts; the cause-and-effect table and acid-rain frame are in `environment.md`.

**Deforestation** — clearing large areas of forest by cutting or burning trees, for wood and other raw materials and to free land for human use. It leads to:
- **Soil erosion** — no roots hold the soil, so rain washes and wind blows away the nutrient-rich topsoil. New plants struggle to grow, soil organisms die, landslides can kill people and destroy buildings. Soil washed into rivers makes the water cloudy; sunlight cannot reach water plants for photosynthesis, and plants and animals die from polluted water and too little dissolved oxygen.
- **Global warming** (with burning).

**Global warming** — the rise in the average temperature of the Earth's surface. **Greenhouse gases** such as carbon dioxide build up and trap heat from the Sun (the **greenhouse effect**). Effects: ice caps melt, sea levels rise and low-lying land floods; weather patterns change, with droughts and floods; plant growth suffers, leading to food shortages.

**Pollution**

| Kind | Causes | Effects |
|---|---|---|
| **Air** | Dust and smoke from burning; gases from vehicles and factories | Respiratory problems in people and animals; some pollutants dissolve in rainwater as **acid rain**, which corrodes buildings and structures and kills aquatic life when water turns too acidic |
| **Water** | Household waste water; untreated chemical waste from industry; rubbish dumped in water; oil spills from ships and tankers; fertiliser run-off | Oil harms marine life and sea birds; plastic litter chokes, entangles and poisons aquatic animals; fertilisers cause too much plant and algae growth, which lowers oxygen in the water and blocks sunlight from submerged plants |
| **Land** | Non-biodegradable materials (plastics) that decomposers cannot easily break down, lasting hundreds of years; overuse of fertilisers, pesticides and weed killers; factory chemical waste; irresponsible dumping | Exposed rubbish dumps are unsightly and unhygienic, and breed disease |

## Adaptations — P6 (Standard and Foundation)

An **adaptation** is a feature or behaviour that helps an organism **survive** in its environment.
- **Structural** — a special body part or structure (long hind legs, thick fur, hooked beak).
- **Behavioural** — a special way of acting (huddling, playing dead, migrating, hunting in packs).

Adaptations serve to: cope with physical factors (extreme temperatures), obtain food, escape predators, reproduce by finding and attracting mates or dispersing seeds and fruits, move, and (for animals) get enough air. Seed and fruit dispersal is taught under Cycles (reproduction); here the focus is only recognising the fruit's physical features as structural adaptations.

Every adaptation answer ends at **survival**, through the mechanism. The frames for spread-out and needle leaves, camouflage, mimicry, and cold- and hot-place adaptations are in `environment.md` — use them.

### Plants getting sunlight

| Adaptation | How it helps |
|---|---|
| Leaves at the bottom of the canopy are larger than those at the top | Larger surface area traps more of the limited light for photosynthesis |
| Leaves at the top are smaller | In direct sun, a smaller surface area means less water lost (*stomata* — term not required; *transpiration* is not a syllabus term either; say *less water vapour is lost from the leaves*) |
| Plants growing on tall trees (fern, orchid) | Reach more sunlight |
| Climbers with weak stems (money plant, morning glory) | Climb a support to reach more sunlight |
| Leaves arranged so they do not block each other (e.g. alternate) | Each leaf traps enough light |
| Creepers spread over the ground (strawberry, watermelon) | Cover a larger area to trap enough light |
| Floating plants with air spaces in leaf stalks (water hyacinth) | Stay afloat on the surface to trap sunlight |
| Submerged plants with air spaces in leaves and stems (cabomba, elodea) | Stay upright to get more light |

### Coping with extreme temperatures

| Cold | Hot |
|---|---|
| Arctic fox — small ears: less exposed surface area, less heat lost | Fennec fox — large ears: more exposed surface area, loses heat faster |
| Polar bear — thick fur traps air, a poor conductor of heat, so less heat lost | Sidewinder snake — moves sideways, less body in contact with hot sand, less heat gained |
| Seal, walrus — thick blubber under the skin reduces heat loss | Camel — long legs keep the body away from hot sand; padded feet spread its weight and protect from heat |
| Penguins — huddle: less exposed surface area, less heat lost (behavioural) | Gerbil — stays underground in the heat, active in the evening (behavioural) |
| Swallows — migrate to warmer places (behavioural) | Bat — active only at night when cooler (behavioural) |
| Squirrel — hibernates in winter: breathing and life processes slow down to save energy (behavioural) | Saguaro cactus — long, deep roots reach underground water; barrel cactus — fleshy stem stores water; prickly pear — needle-like leaves reduce water loss |

### Predators obtaining food

- Tiger, leopard — striped or spotted coat camouflages them among tall plants in dim light, so prey is less likely to spot them; snowy owl — colour matches its surroundings.
- Anglerfish — a glowing tip on its head lures prey in the dark deep sea.
- Wolf — hunts in packs to catch large prey (behavioural); keen hearing and sight to locate prey; sharp pointed canine teeth to grip and tear flesh.

**Beaks and feet**

| Animal | Structure | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Eagle | Strong, sharp, hooked beak; long curved claws (talons) | Tear flesh; catch and grip prey |
| Pelican | Long bill with a throat pouch; webbed feet | Scoop up fish; paddle and swim |
| Woodpecker | Hard pointed beak; toes with sharp claws pointing different ways | Dig insects out of wood; grip tree trunks and balance |
| Ostrich | Broad beak; long muscular two-toed legs | Feed on plants and small animals; run fast and kick predators |
| Jacana | Long toes and claws | Spread its weight to walk on floating plants |
| Chicken | Small pointed beak; short sharp claws | Peck and scratch the ground for food |

### Protection from predators

- **Camouflage** — blending with the surroundings so predators are **less likely** to spot the animal (and predators less likely to be spotted by prey): stick insect and leaf insect shaped and coloured like twigs and leaves; chameleon and squid change colour.
- **Mimicry** — a harmless king snake looks like the venomous coral snake, so predators avoid it.
- **Eyespots** — butterflyfish's fake eyespots confuse predators; elephant hawk-moth caterpillar's large eye markings scare them off.
- **Special body covering** — turtle's hard shell, pangolin's hard scales, porcupine's sharp quills.
- **Looking bigger** — puffer fish inflates to look bigger and more frightening.
- **Playing dead** — opossum (behavioural).
- **Living in large groups** — zebras, deer, elephants, buffaloes, schools of fish: more eyes watch for predators, the group defends itself, and a predator finds it hard to single one out (behavioural).
- **Escaping** — jerboa's long hind legs let it leap away.

### Attracting mates

- Bright colours on males — guppy, peacock.
- Males fighting for a mate — deer, narwhal.
- Calls and displays — bullfrog inflates and calls; frigate bird inflates a red throat sac and calls; birds-of-paradise and peacocks dance.
- Light — fireflies glow in the dark; some squid glow blue.
- Scent — female moths and bees release a substance; the male moth's hairy antennae detect even a trace.

### Breathing in water

| Animal | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| Fish, tadpole | Gills take in oxygen dissolved in water |
| Crab, mudskipper | Gills, plus chambers that hold oxygen-rich water so they can stay out of water a short time |
| Lungfish | Gills in water, lung-like structures on land |
| Frog, flatworm | Moist skin takes in dissolved oxygen; frogs also use lungs on land |
| Mosquito larva and pupa, water scorpion | Breathing tubes reach above the surface for air |
| Water beetle, water spider | Hairy bodies or wings trap air bubbles to breathe underwater and hunt longer |
| Dolphin, whale | Blowhole on top of the head; hold their breath for long periods |
| Seal, walrus, dugong | Push nostrils above water; close nostrils underwater |
| Crocodile, alligator | Nostrils on top of the head breathe while the body is submerged; nose flaps close underwater |

### Movement

| Medium | Examples |
|---|---|
| Air | Eagle — streamlined body (to move through air more easily; *air resistance* not required), hollow strong light bones, powerful flight muscles, feathers that trap air to keep flight muscles warm; bat — skin membrane wings with large surface area; insects — one or two pairs of wings, muscles that beat them rapidly to reach food and escape |
| Water | Seal and turtle — flippers; duck, otter, beaver — webbed feet to paddle; water strider — tiny hairs on its legs trap air so it stands on the water surface |
| Land | Kangaroo — muscular hind legs to hop; squirrel — longer hind legs to leap between trees, long tail to balance; deer — hooves to run long distances; mole — backward claws for digging; camel — padded feet do not sink in sand; koala — grasping hands for branches; snake — scales grip the ground |

## Beyond the current syllabus

Commonly taught alongside these topics but not a learning outcome in `science.md`. Do not set it, and do not mark a child down for not knowing it.

- **Magnetic shielding** — "magnetism passes through non-magnetic materials such as cardboard but not through magnetic materials such as an iron sheet". `science.md` records magnetic shielding as not required at P3.
- **Demagnetising** — dropping, heating or hammering a magnet repeatedly weakens it. No outcome.
- **Strength of an electromagnet** — more batteries or more coils of wire give a stronger electromagnet. Not a P3 outcome (P3 only makes a magnet by the electrical method); keep it for enrichment.
- **Lodestone** — a naturally occurring magnet. The syllabus only says magnets can be made of iron or steel.
- **Weight on the Moon and the newton** — a 60 kg child weighs about 600 N on Earth but far less on the Moon, while mass stays 60 kg; 1 kg ≈ 10 N. The syllabus asks only that weight comes from gravity.
- **About 10 % of energy** passes to the next organism in a food chain — the figure is beyond P6.
