## Light — P4

**Sources of light** — a source of light gives off its own light.

| Natural sources | Man-made sources |
|---|---|
| Sun, stars, lightning, firefly | table lamp, candle, traffic light |

- **The Moon is not a source of light.** It does not give off its own light; it reflects light from the Sun into our eyes. A common confusion.

**Light travels in straight lines.**
- Set-up: three cards, each with a small hole, stand in a row between a lit candle and an eye. When the three holes are lined up in a straight line, the candle flame can be seen. When one card is shifted so the holes are not in a straight line, the flame cannot be seen.

**Light can be reflected; how we see.**
- An object is seen when it is a source of light, or when it **reflects light** from a source into our eyes. A bird in sunlight: light from the Sun falls on the bird, the bird reflects it into our eyes, and we see the bird.
- Two mirrors set at the two bends of a Z-shaped tube (a periscope) reflect light from an object around the bends into the eye.
- The law of reflection (angles) is **not required** at P4 — keep to "the object reflects light into our eyes".

**Shadows**
- Light travels in straight lines, so a **shadow** forms when light is completely or partially blocked by an object.
- Different materials let different amounts of light through, and that sets how dark the shadow is:

| How much light passes through | Examples | Shadow |
|---|---|---|
| **Lets most light through** | clear glass, clear plastic, cling wrap | no shadow (or a very faint one) |
| **Lets some light through** | frosted glass, tracing paper, shower curtain | light shadow |
| **Lets no light through** | wooden chair, stone, book, metal bottle | dark shadow |

- The terms (*transparent / translucent / opaque* — not required at P3 or P4; say *lets most / some / no light through*). Many classrooms still teach the three words; a child may use them, but a question or mark scheme must not demand them.

**Variables that change a shadow** (the investigation the syllabus names: shape, size and position of object; distance light source–object and object–screen)

| Change | Effect on shadow |
|---|---|
| Object moved nearer the screen (further from the light) | shadow **smaller** |
| Object moved nearer the light (further from the screen) | shadow **bigger** |
| Object turned to a different orientation, or light moved to a different position | shadow can take a **different shape** — e.g. a cylinder casts a rectangle side-on and a circle end-on |

- Shadows outdoors through the day: sunlight falls on an object from different angles at different times, so the shadow's length and direction change. **Shortest at noon** when the Sun is directly overhead; **longest at sunrise and sunset**; the shadow points away from the Sun (westward in the morning, eastward in the evening). Treat this as an application of "position of the light source". The cause — the Earth rotating on its axis so that the Sun appears to move from east to west — is beyond the current syllabus; see the last section.
- Fair-test set-up: keep the light source, object and screen fixed except for the one distance being changed; measure the height (or area) of the shadow on the screen.

## Heat — P4

**Heat and its sources**
- **Heat is a form of energy.** It cannot be seen but can be felt.
- A **heat source** is anything that produces heat. Most sources of light are also sources of heat.

| Kind | Examples |
|---|---|
| Natural | Sun, volcano, geyser |
| Burning | candles; fuels such as coal, petrol, wood |
| Man-made (electrical appliances) | iron, toaster, hair-dryer, oven |
| Friction | rubbing two objects against each other |

**Temperature**
- **Temperature** is a measurement of an object's **degree of hotness**, measured in **degrees Celsius (°C)**.
- Instruments: a **laboratory thermometer** for experiments (range about −10 °C to 100 °C); a **clinical** thermometer (about 35 °C to 42 °C) or **digital** thermometer (reads to one decimal place) for body temperature; a **datalogger with a temperature sensor** for recording over time.

**Heat versus temperature** — the distinction the syllabus asks a child to draw:

| Same temperature, different amount | Same amount, different temperature |
|---|---|
| 50 ml and 100 ml of water, both at 60 °C | 100 ml at 80 °C and 100 ml at 40 °C |
| The 100 ml holds **more heat** — more heat was needed to warm the larger volume to 60 °C | The 80 °C water holds **more heat** — more heat was needed to warm it to the higher temperature |

So the amount of heat in a substance depends on how much of it there is and on its temperature; the thermometer reads only the temperature.

**Heat flows from a hotter to a colder object/region until both reach the same temperature.**
- Set-up: a steel rod heated at one end, with thumbtacks A, B, C stuck along it with wax, A nearest the flame. Result: A drops first, then B, then C — heat flows along the rod away from the flame.
- The temperature of an object rises when it **gains** heat and falls when it **loses** heat.

**Expansion and contraction**
- Matter (solid, liquid, gas) **expands** — increases in volume — when it **gains heat**, and **contracts** — decreases in volume — when it **loses heat**.
- **Mass stays the same** during expansion or contraction; only the volume changes. A common confusion.

Experiments:

| Set-up | Observation | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Metal ball that just passes through a metal ring | after heating, the ball cannot pass through; after cooling, it passes through again | the solid expanded on heating and contracted on cooling |
| Flask of coloured water, stoppered, with a glass tube; flask heated | level in the tube **first drops slightly**, then rises; after cooling it falls back to the start | the glass flask gains heat and expands first, giving the water more room; then the water expands. On cooling, the water contracts |
| Flask of air, tube dipping into a beaker of water; flask heated | air bubbles out of the tube into the water | the air expands, takes up more space, and some is pushed out |
| Same flask; cloth at 0 °C wrapped round it | water is drawn up into the tube | the air contracts, takes up less space, and water moves in |
| Balloon over the mouth of an empty bottle; bottle in hot water / in ice | balloon inflates in hot water, deflates in ice | air gains heat and expands / loses heat and contracts |
| Dented ping-pong ball in hot water | dent pops out | air inside gains heat, expands and pushes the shell back out |
| Test tube of air with a drop of coloured water in a narrow tube; tube held in warm hands / in ice water | drop moves up / down | air gains heat and expands / loses heat and contracts |

Everyday applications — each one leaves room for expansion or uses contraction:

| Application | How it works |
|---|---|
| Gaps between railway track sections | room for the rails to expand on hot days, so they do not buckle |
| Gaps between concrete pavement slabs | room to expand and contract, so the surface does not crack |
| Curved joints / coils in pipes carrying hot liquids or gases | room to expand and contract, so the pipes do not break |
| One end of a bridge on a movable joint | room to expand on hot days and contract on cold days |
| Overhead cables hung loosely | room to contract on cold days without snapping — but not too loose, or they sag dangerously on hot days |
| Bottles and drink cans not filled to the top | room for the liquid to expand, so the container does not burst |
| Fitting a metal tyre onto a wheel | the tyre is heated so it expands and slips on; as it loses heat it contracts and grips the wheel tightly |
| Bimetallic strip (e.g. in a fire alarm) | a strip of two different metals bonded together; on heating one metal expands more than the other, so the strip bends and closes (or opens) a circuit |

- Bimetallic strip: the bending comes from the two metals **expanding by different amounts**, not from one being a better conductor of heat — a confusion some classroom notes carry. Recall of how fast specific metals transfer heat is **not required**, so keep a question to "metal X expands more than metal Y" given in the stem.

**Uneven expansion can crack a material.** It happens when one surface expands more than another, or one expands while the other contracts.
- Boiling water poured into a thick glass: glass is a poor conductor of heat, so the inner surface gains heat and expands faster than the outer surface; the uneven expansion cracks the glass. A cold thick glass stood in boiling water cracks the same way, outer surface first.
- A pavement on warm soil when the air suddenly turns cold: the top loses heat to the cold air and contracts while the bottom gains heat from the warm soil and expands; the pavement cracks.

**Good and poor conductors of heat**
- **Good conductors** let heat flow through them easily: **metals**.
- **Poor conductors** do not: **wood, plastics, air, rubber**.
- Comparing conductors in a set-up: rods of materials A, B, C heated at one end with wax at the other — the wax that takes **longest** to melt sits on the **poorest** conductor, the best choice for keeping ice-cream cold.
- A steel spoon in hot water lets the water cool to room temperature faster than a plastic spoon does — steel conducts heat from the hot water to the surroundings faster.
- **Air is a poor conductor**, so trapped air slows heat flow:

| Example | Why it keeps things warm (or cool) |
|---|---|
| Birds fluffing up feathers in cold weather | feathers trap more air, so the body loses heat to the cold surroundings more slowly |
| Thick fur of polar bears, thick feathers of penguins | a layer of trapped air next to the skin slows heat loss |
| Double-walled glass with an air space | hot water inside loses heat more slowly than in a single-walled glass |
| Flask wrapped in wool | wool traps air; the water cools more slowly than in an unwrapped flask |
| Double-pane windows and hollow bricks | trapped air slows heat loss from the house in cold weather and heat gain into it in hot weather |

Chains for all of these — *conducts heat from … to … at a slower rate* — are in `heat-and-matter.md`; use them rather than "air keeps the heat in".

## Photosynthesis — P6 (Standard; Foundation narrower)

Foundation's only Core Idea here is *the Sun is our primary source of energy (light and heat)*; both streams share the Practice of investigating the requirements for photosynthesis. Respiration and how plants and animals differ in obtaining energy are **Standard only**.

**Photosynthesis** — green plants use **light energy** to make **sugar** from **carbon dioxide** and **water**; **oxygen** is given out.

- Word equation as commonly taught: *carbon dioxide + water → (light, chlorophyll) → sugar + oxygen*.
- **Requirements**: light, carbon dioxide, water (and chlorophyll, the green substance in the leaf that absorbs light). **Products**: sugar and oxygen.
- Water (with dissolved mineral salts) is absorbed by the roots and carried up by the **water-carrying tubes**; sugar made in the leaves is carried to other parts of the plant by the **food-carrying tubes**. (*xylem / phloem* — not required; say *water-carrying / food-carrying tubes*.)
- The Sun is the primary source of energy for living things; plants make their own food using light energy, animals obtain energy by eating plants or other animals (Standard: *differentiate between the ways plants and animals obtain energy*).

**Investigating the requirements** — classroom method: excess sugar is stored in the leaf as **starch**, so a leaf that has photosynthesised turns **blue-black** when tested with **iodine solution**; a leaf that has not stays **brown**. (The syllabus names sugar and oxygen as the products; starch and the iodine test are the classroom route to detecting that photosynthesis happened, not a named outcome.)

| Variable tested | Set-up | Result | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Light | part of a leaf on a plant is covered with black paper, then the plant is left in sunlight; the leaf is tested with iodine | covered part stays brown; uncovered part turns blue-black | light is needed |
| Carbon dioxide | two plants under bell jars in sunlight; plant A's jar also holds a dish of a solution that absorbs carbon dioxide | A's leaf stays brown; B's leaf turns blue-black | carbon dioxide is needed |

- Fair test: everything identical except the one requirement removed — same plant type and size, same light, same time.
- Another common read-out is the oxygen given off (bubbles from a water plant): more light → faster photosynthesis → more bubbles.

**Respiration — Standard only**
- Living things break down **digested food** in the presence of **oxygen** to **release energy** for life processes (growth, movement, reproduction); carbon dioxide (and water) are given out. The syllabus's focus is the **release of energy from food** — do not go further into the chemistry.
- During exercise the body needs more energy, so the rate of respiration rises; breathing rate rises to take in more oxygen and remove carbon dioxide faster; heart rate rises so blood carries digested food and oxygen to the cells faster.

| Respiration | Photosynthesis |
|---|---|
| breaks down food to release energy | uses light energy to make food |
| all living things | green plants only |
| all the time, day and night | only when there is light |
| uses oxygen, gives out carbon dioxide | uses carbon dioxide, gives out oxygen |

| Breathing | Respiration |
|---|---|
| taking in oxygen and giving out carbon dioxide (gas exchange) | breaking down digested food with oxygen to release energy |

- Common confusion: plants respire all the time too, not only animals; and breathing is not respiration.

## Energy conversion — P6 Standard only

Foundation does not meet this topic at all.

**The required forms** are exactly six: **kinetic, potential, light, electrical, sound, heat**.

| Form | Note |
|---|---|
| **Kinetic energy** | energy a moving object has |
| **Potential energy** | stored energy — in a raised object, a stretched or compressed spring, food, fuels, batteries |
| **Light energy** | lets us see; needed by plants for photosynthesis |
| **Electrical energy** | carried by electric current; generated in power stations and converted to many useful forms |
| **Sound energy** | produced by a vibrating object; what we hear |
| **Heat energy** | makes things hot or warm |

- (*gravitational potential energy*, *chemical potential energy*, *elastic potential energy* — not required; say *potential energy*.) Many classroom notes split potential energy into these three; carry the idea (a raised object, food/fuel/battery, a stretched spring all store potential energy) without the terms.
- "Wind energy", "solar energy", "water energy" name **sources**, not forms. In an answer, write the form: *kinetic energy of the moving air*, *light energy from the Sun*.

**Energy sources**
- Energy from most of our energy resources comes in some way from the **Sun**.

| Renewable | Non-renewable |
|---|---|
| can be replenished within a short time after use; available indefinitely | cannot be replenished within a short time; used up faster than they form, so limited |
| wind, solar (the Sun), moving water (dams, tides) | **fossil fuels** — coal, petroleum (oil), natural gas — formed over millions of years from the remains of plants and animals |

- Burning fossil fuels causes air pollution and releases greenhouse gases that add to **global warming** (the same idea appears under man's impact in Interactions within the environment).
- The Sun is a clean source: using it gives off no greenhouse gases, and it will last far into the future.

**Conversion chains** — write each form with the object holding it, source form first (frame in `energy-forces-and-electricity.md`).

| Device or event | Conversion |
|---|---|
| Hydroelectric dam | potential energy of water held high behind the dam → kinetic energy of falling water → kinetic energy of the turning turbine → electrical energy from the generator |
| Wind turbine | kinetic energy of moving air → kinetic energy of the spinning rotor blades → electrical energy from the generator |
| Tidal generator | kinetic energy of the tidal current → kinetic energy of the spinning blades and turbine → electrical energy from the generator |
| Solar panel | light energy from the Sun → electrical energy |
| Pedal-powered phone charger | kinetic energy of the moving legs → kinetic energy of the pedals → electrical energy from the generator → potential energy stored in the phone battery |
| Swinging pendulum | potential energy at the highest point → kinetic energy at the lowest point → potential energy at the other high point |

**Why things never get back to the same height**
- A pendulum released from height X rises only to a lower height Y on the other side; a ball rolled from one rim of a curved ramp cannot reach the same height on the opposite rim; a dropped ball bounces lower each time.
- Reason: on each swing, roll or bounce **some of the kinetic energy is converted to heat and sound energy** (friction with the air and surfaces; the impact with the ground). Less kinetic energy is left to be converted back to potential energy, so the object rises less high.
- Bouncing-ball energy graph as commonly drawn: potential energy is highest at the top of each bounce and lowest at the ground; kinetic energy the reverse; each peak lower than the last.
- Contrast with force (P6 Interactions): the **gravitational force** on the ball — its weight — stays the same whatever its height; only its stored energy changes with height.

**Solar energy in use — a Singapore example**
- A floating solar farm on a reservoir converts light energy to electrical energy. Placing panels on the water also covers part of the water surface, which **reduces the exposed surface area** and so reduces water lost by evaporation (link to P5 Water, Standard).
- The water also cools the panels: water gains heat from the warm panels and evaporates, so the panels lose heat; wind speeds up the evaporation. Panels work more efficiently when cooler, so more electricity is produced than from rooftop panels.

## Beyond the current syllabus

Commonly taught in classrooms but with no outcome behind it in `../science.md`. Do not set it as a requirement or mark a child down for not knowing it; where a question uses it, everything needed must be given in the stem.

- **Why the Sun appears to move** — the Earth rotates on its axis, so the Sun appears to cross the sky from east to west. Earth-and-space ideas are not in this syllabus.
- **Other factors in the rate of heat transfer** — beyond the type of conductor:
  - **Contact / exposed surface area**: the larger the area in contact with the heat source or surroundings, the faster heat is gained or lost. A flat-bottomed pot in full contact with a hot plate boils its water sooner than one raised on ridges; an elephant's larger ears lose heat to the surroundings faster.
  - **Colour and texture**: black, dull, rough surfaces absorb heat (from the Sun) faster; white, shiny, smooth surfaces absorb less and reflect more. A black tin in sunlight warms faster than a white shiny one; foil wrapping keeps chocolate from melting; a penguin's black back absorbs heat from the Sun.
- **What kinetic and potential energy depend on** — kinetic energy is greater for a heavier or faster object; the potential energy of a raised object is greater the heavier it is and the higher it is; a spring stores more the more it is stretched or compressed. The syllabus asks only that the forms be recognised and exemplified.
- **Colour of light and photosynthesis** — chlorophyll looks green because it reflects most green light, so green light is the least useful for photosynthesis and red or blue light the most.
- **Solar-panel coatings** — panels are coated with anti-reflective material so they absorb more light.
