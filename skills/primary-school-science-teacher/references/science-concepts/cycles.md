## Life cycles of plants — P3

- A **life cycle** is the series of stages a living thing passes through from birth to adult, which then produces young of its own, so the cycle repeats.
- Flowering plant, as a loop: **seed → seedling (young plant) → adult plant**. The adult plant flowers, and fruits and seeds form. Then the cycle repeats.
- The P3 focus is the **stages**: seed, young plant, adult plant. Pollination, fertilisation, seed dispersal and germination are **P5** processes. Teach them in [Reproduction](#reproduction-in-flowering-plants--p5), not here.
- A seedling has a **root**, a **shoot**, **seed leaves** and a **seed coat**.
- Practice: grow a plant from seed over a period of time, observe it, and compare the stages.

## Life cycles of animals — P3

The syllabus limits animal life cycles to **chicken, cockroach, frog, grasshopper, beetle, butterfly, mosquito**. Classify them by the number of stages:

| Animal | Stages | Where the cycle happens | Does the young look like the adult? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chicken | 3: egg → chick → adult | entirely on land | yes |
| Frog | 3: egg → tadpole → adult | partly in water | no |
| Cockroach | 3: egg (in an **egg case**) → nymph → adult | entirely on land | yes (nymph) |
| Grasshopper | 3: egg → nymph → adult | entirely on land | yes (nymph) |
| Butterfly | 4: egg → larva (caterpillar) → pupa → adult | entirely on land | no |
| Beetle (e.g. mealworm beetle) | 4: egg → larva (mealworm) → pupa → adult | entirely on land | no |
| Mosquito | 4: egg → larva → pupa → adult | partly in water (egg, larva and pupa in water) | no |

### The young stages

| | Larva | Pupa | Nymph |
|---|---|---|---|
| Looks like the adult? | no | no | yes, but smaller and **wingless** |
| Feeds? | yes, eats a lot | **does not feed or move**; the adult body develops inside | yes |
| Moults? | yes, several times before becoming a pupa | no | yes, several times before becoming an adult |

- **Moulting** — the young sheds its hard outer covering so that it can grow bigger. Asked to *name and describe* it: "Moulting. The nymph sheds its outer covering and grows larger after each moult."
- A nymph cannot fly because its wings are not fully developed; the adult can.
- Singular and plural: larva/larvae, pupa/pupae; *larval* and *pupal* stage.

### Frog: young and adult in different places

- Tadpole lives only in water; the adult frog lives in water and on land.
- Why that helps: the young and adult **do not compete with each other for resources such as food**, so more young survive; and if conditions become unfavourable where one lives, the other can still survive, so the frog's kind continues.

### Butterfly

- The larva (caterpillar) feeds on leaves, so it is a **pest** at the larval stage.
- The adult feeds on nectar and helps pollinate flowers.

### Mosquito

- Eggs are laid in **stagnant water**; larva and pupa live in water and take in air at the surface through **breathing tubes**.
- The adult is the hardest stage to kill because it can fly away. Egg, larva and pupa cannot fly, so they are the stages to target.
- Control, each tied to a stage:
  - **Remove stagnant water**, e.g. empty flowerpot plates, overturn pails, change vase water, keep roof gutters clear. No stagnant water means nowhere to lay eggs.
  - **Pour a layer of oil on stagnant water**. Larvae and pupae cannot take in air through their breathing tubes, so they die.

### Comparing two life cycles

Give organisms X (egg case → nymph → adult) and Y (egg → larva → pupa → adult):

- **Similarity** — both have an egg stage.
- **Difference** — X has a 3-stage life cycle; Y has a 4-stage life cycle.
- **Why lay many eggs** — some eggs are eaten by predators. Laying many means some still hatch and grow into adults, so the organism's kind survives.

## Matter — P4

- **Matter** is anything that has **mass** and **occupies space**.
- Demonstrations, each described:
  - **Air has mass.** Hang two equal balloons from a balance beam, and it is level. Let the air out of one, and the beam tilts down on the side of the balloon that is still full.
  - **Air occupies space.** In a flask with a funnel sealed into its neck, water drips in only slowly, because air inside takes up the space. Push a glass mouth-down into water, and the water does not fill it because air is trapped inside. A balloon inside a bottle cannot be blown up, because the air in the bottle takes up the space.
  - **Solids occupy space.** Drop a marble into a full flask with a side tube, and water flows out into a beaker.
- **Not matter** — light, heat, sound, shadow, thunder (a sound) and lightning (light). They have no mass and take up no space.

### Three states

Write *fixed*, not *definite*.

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed shape? | yes | no (takes the shape of its container) | no (takes the shape of its container) |
| Fixed volume? | yes | yes | no (fills the whole container) |
| Can be compressed? | no | no | yes |
| Examples | chair, table, pencil, ruler | water, oil, milk, alcohol | oxygen, carbon dioxide, nitrogen, water vapour |

- Evidence each way: a block keeps its shape in any container. 100 ml of water poured from a conical flask into a beaker is still 100 ml. In a sealed syringe, a plunger pushed against solid or water does not move, but pushed against air it does.
- **Volume of air in a closed container** = capacity of the container − the volume of the solids and liquids inside. For example, an 800 cm³ container with 200 cm³ of water holds 600 cm³ of air. Pumping in another 100 cm³ of air leaves it at 600 cm³, because the air compresses. The full frame is in [`../science-answering/heat-and-matter.md`](../science-answering/heat-and-matter.md).

### Measuring mass and volume

| | Mass | Volume |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | the amount of matter in an object | the amount of space an object occupies |
| Units | kilogram (kg), gram (g) | cubic centimetre (cm³), litre (l), millilitre (ml) |
| Apparatus | beam balance; electronic scale | measuring cylinder; displacement can |

- **Beam balance**: a level beam means equal mass, and the side that tilts down holds the greater mass. An **electronic scale** shows the mass on its screen.
- **Volume of an irregular solid**, by the water it displaces:
  - **Measuring cylinder**: read the water level, sink the object, read again. Volume = final − initial, e.g. 40 cm³ − 20 cm³ = 20 cm³.
  - **Displacement can**: fill the can to the spout, lower the object in, and collect the overflow in a measuring cylinder. The volume of the object is the volume of water collected.
- Common confusion: an object that **floats** does not displace its whole volume, so neither method gives its volume directly.

## Reproduction in flowering plants — P5

**Standard** describes the four processes; **Foundation** only *states* them. Fertilisation at Foundation is defined as "the process that leads to the development of fruits and seeds".

### Parts of a flower

The flower diagram is a cut-away view. The **stamens** sit in a ring, each a **filament** topped by an **anther**. The central **pistil** has a **stigma** on top, then a **style**, then a swollen **ovary** at the base, holding **ovules**. **Petals** surround these parts.

| Part | Male / female | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Anther | male | produces pollen grains |
| Filament | male | holds the anther up |
| Stigma | female | receives pollen grains |
| Style | female | connects the stigma to the ovary |
| Ovary | female | holds and protects the ovules; becomes the **fruit** after fertilisation |
| Ovule | female | becomes the **seed** after fertilisation |
| Petal | — | often brightly coloured, to attract animals such as insects and birds that pollinate |

### The four processes, in cycle order

Seed → **germination** → seedling → young plant → adult plant with flowers → **pollination** and **fertilisation** → adult plant with fruits → **seed dispersal** → seed.

### Pollination

- **Pollination** is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther to the stigma of the same flower, or of another flower of the same kind.
- Do not use *self-pollination* or *cross-pollination*. Those terms are not required; describe the transfer instead.

| | Animal-pollinated | Wind-pollinated |
|---|---|---|
| Flower | usually large, brightly coloured, scented | usually small, dull, no scent |
| Nectar | present | absent |
| Anthers | inside the flower | hang out of the flower, so the wind shakes pollen free |
| Stigma | sticky, so pollen sticks when an animal brushes past | feathery, so it catches more pollen from the air |
| How | an animal visits for nectar; pollen sticks to its body; it brushes the pollen onto the stigma of the next flower | filaments sway; pollen is carried by the wind onto the stigma of another flower of the same kind |

### Fertilisation

- **Fertilisation** is when a male reproductive cell (from the pollen grain) **fuses** with a female reproductive cell (in the ovule).
- After fertilisation, the petals, style and stigma usually wither and drop off. The **ovules develop into seeds**, and the **ovary enlarges into a fruit**.
- How the male cell reaches the ovule: a classroom diagram often draws a **pollen tube** growing down the style. Pollen tube formation is not required at Standard, and Foundation does not reach it at all. Say *the male reproductive cell from the pollen grain fuses with the female reproductive cell in the ovule*.
- For the full flower-to-fruit chain, see [`../science-answering/living-things.md`](../science-answering/living-things.md).

### Seed dispersal

- **Why disperse away from the parent** — to prevent overcrowding and reduce competition with the parent plant for water, nutrients, light and space.

| Dispersed by | Characteristics of the fruit or seed | How it helps | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wind | small, light, with wing-like or parachute-like structures | carried by the wind over long distances | dandelion, maple, shorea, African tulip, angsana |
| Water | waterproof outer layer, or fibrous husk that traps air | floats and is carried by water currents over long distances | coconut, pong pong, lotus, cattail |
| Splitting (explosive action) | fruit wall dries and splits open suddenly when ripe | flings seeds out in different directions around the parent | balsam, saga, bean, rubber |
| Animals, edible | juicy, fragrant or brightly coloured fruit | animals eat it; indigestible seeds pass out in droppings elsewhere | apple, cherry, kiwi |
| Animals, inedible | hooks or stiff hairs | cling to an animal's fur and are carried away | love grass, mimosa |

**Reading dispersal patterns.** A map shows a parent plant and where its seeds landed:

| Pattern | Dispersal method |
|---|---|
| seeds spread on one side, along the wind direction | wind |
| seeds along a stream, in the direction of the water flow | water |
| seeds clustered close around the parent | splitting |
| seeds scattered randomly, far and near | animals, which move about randomly |

Fruits and seeds as **structural adaptations** for dispersal are revisited at P6 under Interactions.

### Germination

- **Germination** is the growth of a seed into a seedling. A seed needs **water, oxygen and warmth** to germinate.
- The **root** appears first, then the **shoot**.
- At first the seedling uses food stored in its **seed leaves**, so the seed leaves shrink. When the **true leaves** appear and turn green, the plant can make its own food. How it does this, photosynthesis, is a P6 topic.
- Graph of mass over the first days: the **seed leaves lose mass** as their stored food is used up, and the **seedling gains mass** as it grows.
- When the plant matures, it flowers. Fertilised flowers develop into fruits and seeds.

### Spores and seeds

The syllabus also has the Practice *investigate the ways in which plants reproduce — spores, seeds* (Standard), or *observe and compare* them (Foundation). These notes have no material on spores. Work from `science.md`.

## Reproduction in humans — P5

Standard *recognises* the process of fertilisation; Foundation *states* it. What `science.md` requires: **ovaries produce eggs**, **testes produce sperms**, fertilisation occurs when a **sperm fuses with an egg**, and the **fertilised egg develops in the womb**.

| Part | Function |
|---|---|
| Testes (singular *testis*) | produce sperms, the male reproductive cells |
| Ovaries | produce eggs, the female reproductive cells |
| Womb | where the fertilised egg develops |

- Classroom diagrams also label the penis, vagina and fallopian tubes. `science.md` names none of these, so do not set them as required content.
- **Fallopian tube as the site of fertilisation** — not required: *the specific location where fertilisation takes place in the female reproductive system is not required*. Say *a sperm fuses with an egg*.
- **Implantation, foetal development, the umbilical cord** — not required at Standard or Foundation. Stop at *the fertilised egg develops in the womb*.
- Some notes define fertilisation as the fusion of the **nuclei** of sperm and egg. The syllabus wording is the fusion of the reproductive cells. Mark either, but teach *the sperm fuses with the egg*.

**Similarity with flowering plants** (Standard only): in both, fertilisation is the fusion of a male reproductive cell with a female reproductive cell, and it leads to a new individual.

## Heredity — P5 Standard only

Foundation has no outcome on continuity of kind or passing on characteristics.

- Living things **reproduce to ensure the continuity of their kind**.
- **Heredity** is the passing on of characteristics from parents to their young.
- **Inherited physical traits** (paired forms):
  - V-shaped or straight hairline
  - able or unable to roll the tongue
  - naturally straight or curly hair
  - freckles or none
  - bent or straight little finger
  - hitchhiker's or straight thumb
  - attached or detached earlobe
  - dimples or none
- **Not inherited**: fingerprints, the length of hair or hairstyle, the length of fingernails. These are not passed from parents.
- **Family tree** conventions (classroom, for reading questions):
  - a square is a male; a circle is a female
  - a horizontal line joining them is a married couple
  - a line branching down from it leads to their children

## Water and changes of state — P5

**Standard** shows an understanding of the changes; **Foundation** states them and the melting and boiling points. The two investigations below (heating and cooling curves, and rate of evaporation) are **Standard Practices only**.

### The changes

| Change | From → to | Heat | Temperature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Melting** | solid → liquid | gained | at 0 °C (melting point of ice) |
| **Freezing** | liquid → solid | lost | at 0 °C (freezing point of water) |
| **Boiling** | liquid → gas | gained | at 100 °C (boiling point of water) |
| **Evaporation** | liquid → gas | gained | at any temperature below boiling point |
| **Condensation** | gas → liquid | lost | — |

Examples of each state of water: solid (ice, snow, hail), liquid (rain, dew, mist), gas (steam, water vapour).

| | Evaporation | Boiling |
|---|---|---|
| Where | only at the surface of the water | throughout the water and at the surface |
| When | at any temperature below boiling point | only at the boiling point, 100 °C |

### Heating curve — ice heated over a flame (Standard)

The graph plots temperature (°C) against time (minutes). It starts at −19 °C, rises to a flat line at 0 °C, rises again, and flattens at 100 °C.

| Section | What is in the beaker | What is happening |
|---|---|---|
| AB (−19 °C up to 0 °C) | ice only | ice gains heat from the flame; temperature rises |
| BC (flat at 0 °C) | ice and water | **melting**: the heat gained is used to melt the ice, so the temperature stays constant until all the ice has melted |
| CD (0 °C up to 100 °C) | water only | water gains heat; temperature rises |
| DE (flat at 100 °C) | water and steam | **boiling**: the heat gained is used to boil the water, so the temperature stays constant |

### Cooling curve — hot water left out, then put in a freezer (Standard)

The graph starts at 80 °C, falls to a flat line at room temperature, falls again to a flat line at 0 °C, then falls below 0 °C.

| Section | What is in the beaker | What is happening |
|---|---|---|
| PQ (80 °C down to room temperature) | water only | water loses heat to the cooler surrounding air |
| QR (flat at room temperature) | water only | the water is at the same temperature as its surroundings, so there is no net heat gain or loss |
| RS (down to 0 °C, in the freezer) | water only | water loses heat to the cold air in the freezer |
| ST (flat at 0 °C) | water and ice | **freezing**: the temperature stays constant until all the water has frozen |
| TU (below 0 °C) | ice only | ice loses heat; temperature falls |

Read a flat section as a **change of state** (two states present), unless it is at room temperature, which is no change at all.

### Rate of evaporation (Standard)

Three factors. The one-way frames are in [`../science-answering/heat-and-matter.md`](../science-answering/heat-and-matter.md).

| Factor | Effect | Typical set-up |
|---|---|---|
| **Wind** | the stronger the wind, the faster | wet clothes under a fan on high speed against no fan |
| **Exposed surface area** | the larger the exposed surface area of the water, the faster | the same 100 ml of water in a wide dish, a narrow tube and a beaker |
| **Temperature** | the higher the temperature, the faster | clothes drying on a sunny day against a cloudy day |

### Cooling effect of evaporation (Standard)

Water that evaporates **gains heat from** whatever it touches, so that thing **loses heat** and cools. Name both the water and the object losing heat.

- **Wet towel on a feverish child**: the water in the towel gains heat from the body and evaporates. The body loses heat to the water, so body temperature falls.
- **Mist fan cools air more than a plain fan**: the tiny droplets gain heat from the surrounding air and evaporate. The air loses heat to them, so its temperature drops.
- **Swimmer feels cold on leaving the pool**: water on the skin gains heat from the body and evaporates, and the body loses heat. In wind he feels colder still, because the water evaporates faster and takes more heat from the body.
- **Rooftop garden keeps a building cool**: water in the soil and plants gains heat from the surrounding air and evaporates, so the air loses heat and cools.

### Condensation in everyday life (Standard)

Every case is the same three-clause chain: **warmer water vapour comes into contact with a cooler surface, loses heat to it, and condenses into water droplets.** First say where the vapour comes from and why the surface is cooler.

| Observation | Source of water vapour | The cooler surface | Why it is cooler |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bathroom mirror turns cloudy after a warm shower | warm water evaporating | mirror | — |
| Spectacles mist up on leaving an air-conditioned bus | the warm air outside | spectacles | they lost heat to the cold air in the bus |
| Droplets on the outside of a glass of iced drink | surrounding air | outer surface of the glass | the glass lost heat to the cold drink |
| Droplets on a car left out overnight | surrounding air | car's outer surface | it lost heat to the cooler night air |
| Droplets under the lid of a pot of hot soup | water evaporating from the soup | underside of the lid | — |
| "White mist" when breathing out on a cold day | breath | the cold surrounding air | — |
| "White mist" at the spout of a boiling kettle | steam from the spout | the cooler surrounding air | — |
| "White mist" around a popsicle from the freezer | surrounding air | air next to the popsicle | that air lost heat to the popsicle |

Common confusions:

- The **"white mist" is tiny water droplets** (liquid), not steam or water vapour, which cannot be seen.
- Droplets on the **outside** of a cold glass come from the air, not from the drink seeping through.

### The water cycle

Standard explains the **roles of evaporation and condensation**; Foundation recognises the changes of state in it. Both recognise its importance.

- Water in seas, rivers and lakes **gains heat from the Sun and evaporates** into water vapour.
- The water vapour rises, **loses heat to the cooler air higher up and condenses** into tiny water droplets.
- The droplets gather to form **clouds**. As the clouds grow bigger and heavier, the water falls as **rain** back to the land and sea.
- **Importance**: the cycle gives a **continuous supply of fresh water** for living things.

**Getting pure water from sea water.** The set-up is a container of sea water with an empty beaker standing in the middle, covered by a plastic sheet that dips over the beaker's opening, left in the sun.

- **How it works**: water in the sea water gains heat from the Sun and evaporates. The water vapour comes into contact with the cooler inner surface of the plastic sheet, loses heat and condenses into droplets, which drip into the beaker as pure water.
- **Wider or narrower beaker for more water?** Narrower. It leaves the sea water a **larger exposed surface area**, so more water evaporates and more condenses into the beaker.
- **Two other ways to collect more water** without changing the beaker:
  - put ice cubes on the plastic sheet, so the surface is cooler and more vapour condenses
  - heat the sea water, so it evaporates faster

## Beyond the current syllabus

Enrichment that often appears beside the mosquito life cycle. It is no level's content. Use it only as background, never set or mark it:

- The **Aedes** mosquito spreads **dengue fever**. A mosquito that bites an infected person can carry the virus to the next person it bites.
- Male and female mosquitoes differ. The male is usually smaller, feeds only on plant juices such as nectar, and has bushy antennae. The female is larger and feeds on blood, which gives her protein for her eggs. She has a needle-like mouthpart for biting.
- **Releasing male mosquitoes that carry *Wolbachia* bacteria**: females that mate with them lay eggs that do not hatch. Over time there are fewer Aedes mosquitoes and less dengue.
