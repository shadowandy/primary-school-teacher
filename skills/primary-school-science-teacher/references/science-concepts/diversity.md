## Diversity of living and non-living things — P3

### Characteristics of living things

A living thing shows **all** of these. A non-living thing may show one or two (a crystal "grows", a car "moves"), but not all of them.

| Characteristic | What it means | Classroom examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Needs air, food and water to survive** | Take any one away and it dies | Fish in a tank of water with plants; a hamster in a jar with air holes in the lid, and a dish of food and water; a potted plant being watered |
| **Grows** | Gets bigger over time | Chick → hen; seedling → larger plant with more leaves and roots |
| **Reproduces** | Makes young of its own kind | A koala carrying its young; fish, birds and frogs laying eggs |
| **Responds to changes in the environment** | Reacts to something around it | A plant whose leaves fold when touched; a snail pulling back into its shell; a cat reacting to a moving toy; a plant growing towards light |

- *Syllabus wording to hold to:* living things **need water, food and air** to survive, and they **grow, respond and reproduce**. Movement, breathing and getting rid of waste are not on the syllabus list, so do not mark a child down for leaving them out.
- **Practice the syllabus asks for:** observe a mix of living and non-living things and infer how they differ. A good question is "Which of these is living? Give a reason." The reason must be one of the characteristics above.

### Broad groups of living things

The syllabus groups are **plants** (flowering, non-flowering), **animals** (amphibians, birds, fish, insects, mammals, reptiles), **fungi** (mould, mushroom, yeast) and **bacteria**. A child **classifies** by grouping on common characteristics they can observe (`science.md` glossary).

**Boundary:** recall of the names of particular living things and of their own particular characteristics is **not required at P3**. The syllabus's own example is a guppy giving birth to live young. The named examples and exceptions below are there for teaching and recognising. Do not set a question that tests recall of them.

### Classification of animals

| Group | Body covering | Breathes through | Reproduces by | Other features | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Mammals** | Hair or fur | Lungs | Giving birth to young alive | Produce milk and suckle their young. Most live on land, some live in water, and some can fly | Cat, dog, elephant; in water: whale, dolphin, seal, walrus; flying: bat |
| **Birds** | Feathers | Lungs | Laying eggs | A pair of wings and two legs; a beak for feeding; most can fly | Parrot, eagle; non-flying: penguin, ostrich, emu, kiwi |
| **Fish** | Scales | Gills | Laying eggs (most) | Fins for swimming; live in freshwater or the sea | Freshwater: guppy, tilapia; sea: shark, pufferfish |
| **Amphibians** | Moist skin | Young: gills. Adults: lungs and moist skin | Laying eggs | Young live in water, and adults live on land and in water | Frog, toad, salamander, newt |
| **Reptiles** | Dry scales | Lungs | Laying eggs with a leathery shell | | Turtle, crocodile, snake, lizard, iguana |
| **Insects** | Hard outer covering (*exoskeleton*) | Small breathing holes on the body (*spiracles*) | Laying eggs | **Three body parts** (head, thorax, abdomen); **six** jointed legs; most have wings and can fly | Bee, beetle, butterfly, grasshopper |

- *Exoskeleton* and *spiracles* are classroom terms that do not appear in `science.md`. At P3, *hard body covering* and *breathing holes* are enough.
- **Exceptions, for teaching only** (each one is exactly the kind of recall the P3 boundary rules out):
  - Mammals that lay eggs: the spiny anteater and the duck-billed platypus.
  - Fish that give birth to young alive: the guppy, the molly and the swordtail.
  - Insects that cannot fly: the silverfish and the termite.
  - Birds that cannot fly: the penguin, the ostrich, the emu and the kiwi.
- **Classify on the deciding feature, not on looks or habitat:**
  - A **whale or dolphin is a mammal**, not a fish. It breathes through lungs and suckles its young.
  - A **bat is a mammal**, not a bird. It has hair, not feathers, and gives birth to its young alive.
  - A **penguin is a bird**. It has feathers, a beak and lays eggs, even though it cannot fly.
  - **Flying, swimming and living in water belong to no single group**, so on their own they decide nothing.
  - A spider has eight legs and two body parts, so it is **not an insect**.
- **Building a classification key:** split on one observable yes/no question at a time. For example: *Has feathers?* → bird. *Has fins and scales and lives in water?* → fish. *Six legs and three body parts?* → insect. Each split should leave no living thing in both branches.

### Classification of plants

| Group | Bears flowers? | Reproduces by | Where the seeds or spores are | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Flowering plants** | Yes. The flowers develop into fruits | **Seeds** | Inside the fruit | Peach, mangosteen, avocado, banana, blackberry, water lily, lotus |
| **Non-flowering plants** | No | **Spores** | Ferns: in spore cases on the **underside of the leaves**. Mosses: in a **capsule** on a short stalk | Ferns (bird's nest fern, staghorn fern), mosses |

- The **bird's nest fern** shows its spores as lines along the underside of the leaf. The **staghorn fern** grows on tree trunks.
- **Flowering plants have flowers at some point in their lives**, even when no flower is showing. A plant without a flower in the picture is not automatically non-flowering, so look for fruits or seeds too.
- *Reaching ahead:* investigating how plants reproduce by spores and seeds is a **P5** Practice (Cycles, Reproduction). At P3, *spores* and *seeds* are used only to tell the two groups apart.

### Fungi

A mushroom has a **cap**, a **stalk** and, under the cap, thin **gills**. Its **spores** are released from the gills.

| Characteristic | Note |
|---|---|
| Reproduce by **spores** | The same as ferns and mosses, so spores alone do not make something a plant |
| Have **no chlorophyll** and **cannot make their own food** | This is why fungi are a separate group from plants |
| Grow best in **damp, warm, dark** places | Mould on bread kept in a dark, damp cupboard |
| Feed on plants and animals, **dead or alive** | When they feed on dead matter, they break it down into simpler substances, so it **decays** (*decomposition*) |

| Useful | Harmful |
|---|---|
| Edible mushrooms (button, shiitake, enoki) are food | Some mushrooms are poisonous and must not be eaten (for example, a red toadstool with white spots) |
| **Yeast** makes bread dough rise, and is used to make beer and wine | **Mould** growing on food can cause food poisoning |
| Breaking down dead matter returns simpler substances to the soil, which act as fertiliser for plants | Mould damages leather bags and shoes |
| A mould is the source of penicillin, a medicine | Some fungi cause skin infections in humans (ringworm, athlete's foot) |

- The three syllabus examples are **mould, mushroom and yeast**. Penicillin, ringworm and athlete's foot are illustrations. Do not test recall of them at P3.
- *Reaching ahead:* *cannot make its own food* points to photosynthesis (**P6**), and *breaks down dead matter* points to **decomposers** in P6 Interactions within the environment. At P3, state both as facts and do not explain them.

### Bacteria

| Characteristic | Note |
|---|---|
| **Micro-organisms** found everywhere | In air, water, soil, food, and on and inside our bodies |
| Can only be seen **under a microscope** | Much smaller than fungi such as moulds |
| Different types have **different shapes** | Round, rod-shaped, spiral, and some in chains |
| Most **cannot make their own food** | Feed on other living things, dead or alive |
| Break down dead matter into simpler substances (*decomposition*) | Returns nutrients to the soil |

| Useful | Harmful |
|---|---|
| Used to make yogurt and cheese. Some bacteria in yogurt help digestion | Cause sickness and disease (for example, food poisoning) |
| Some bacteria break down oil, and are used to clean up oil spills at sea | Grow on food and make it **turn bad** |
| Break down dead matter and return nutrients to the environment as fertiliser | |

- Named species (Lactobacillus, Salmonella, *E. coli*) are illustrations only. Recall of specific names is not required at P3.
- **Common confusions:**
  - Bacteria and fungi are **both** groups of living things, and neither one is a plant.
  - Mould is a **fungus**, not a bacterium.
  - "Germs" is not a group in the syllabus. Name **bacteria** or **fungi**.

## Diversity of materials — P3

The syllabus focuses on **how a material's properties suit it to a use**. So the typical question is "Why is X made of Y?" or "Which material is best for Z?", not "Recite the properties of Y."

### Properties of materials

The syllabus lists five properties to compare: **strength, flexibility, ability to float/sink in water, waterproof, transparency**. Use its definitions.

| Property | Meaning (syllabus wording where it gives one) | Opposite | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Strength** | Ability to be subjected to loads **without breaking** | Weak: breaks easily under a load | Strong: wood, metal. Weak: paper |
| **Flexibility** | Ability to **bend without breaking** | Stiff: does not bend, and breaks if forced | Flexible: rubber, fabric. Stiff: ceramic |
| **Ability to float** | Whether an object **stays on or near the surface of water** rather than sinking | Sinks: goes to the bottom | Float: styrofoam, cork, most wood. Sink: metal, clay, glass, rock |
| **Waterproof** | The material **does not absorb water** | Absorbent: soaks up water | Waterproof: plastic, rubber, glass. Absorbent: fabric, paper |
| **Transparency** | How much light the material **allows to pass through**: most, some or none | | Lets most light through: clear glass. Lets some light through: frosted glass. Lets no light through: wood, metal, rubber |

**Boundaries that bind here:**
- The terms ***transparent / translucent / opaque*** are **not required at P3** (or at P4). Say *allows most light / some light / no light to pass through*. Classroom notes often teach the three terms. Do not use them in a P3 question or model answer, and do not penalise a child for not knowing them.
- **Density is not required.** Explain floating as "stays on or near the surface", never as "less dense than water".

### Simple tests (recognising a fair test is the P3-P4 level)

In each test, the **material is the one thing changed**. Keep everything else the same: the size and shape of each strip or ball, the load, the amount of water and the time.

| Property | Set-up | What is observed |
|---|---|---|
| **Strength** | A strip of each material laid across a gap between two blocks, with a load hung from the middle and weights added one at a time | A strong strip holds the load. A weak one breaks. The more weights a strip holds before breaking, the stronger it is |
| **Flexibility** | A strip of each material with the same block pressed onto its middle | A flexible strip bends and springs back without breaking. A stiff one stays flat, or snaps if forced |
| **Waterproof** | Strips of each material stood with their ends in the same depth of water | Water creeps up an absorbent strip, such as fabric. A waterproof strip, such as plastic, stays dry above the water line |
| **Float/sink** | Each object placed gently in a basin of water | It stays on or near the surface (floats), or it drops to the bottom (sinks) |
| **Transparency** | A torch shone at a ball of each material, with a screen behind | Clear glass ball: **no shadow** (it lets most light through). Frosted glass ball: **faint shadow** (lets some light through). Rubber ball: **dark shadow** (lets no light through). The darker the shadow, the less light passes through |

*Reaching ahead:* shadows form because light travels in straight lines and is blocked. That is **P4** Light. At P3, use the shadow only as evidence of how much light got through.

### Types of materials

The seven syllabus materials are **wood, metal, ceramic, rubber, glass, plastic and fabric**.

| Material | Where it comes from | Key properties | Objects, and the property that suits each |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wood** | Natural, from tree trunks | Strong; most wood floats | Wooden bench, door (strong). Paper and cardboard are made from wood |
| **Metal** | Natural, from the ground | Strong | Metal pot, electric wire |
| **Glass** | Man-made, from sand | Breaks easily; most glass lets most light through; waterproof | Window, laboratory apparatus (lets light through, waterproof) |
| **Ceramic** | Man-made, from clay | Stiff; breaks easily | Vase, flower pot, brick, clay pot |
| **Plastic** | Man-made, from crude oil | Waterproof; light; can let most, some or no light through | Bottle, bag, chair; styrofoam cup (floats) |
| **Rubber** | Natural, from latex, the sap of rubber trees | Flexible; waterproof | Gloves, boots, tyre, eraser |
| **Fabric** | Natural (wool from sheep, silk from silkworm cocoons, cotton from the cotton plant) or man-made (nylon) | Flexible; most fabric is absorbent | Clothes, socks, scarf, towel (absorbent) |

- *Brittle* is classroom shorthand for *breaks easily*. Use *breaks easily* in a P3 answer.
- **Conductors of heat and electricity sit in later topics.** Classroom tables often list *metal: good conductor* and *plastic, rubber, fabric: poor conductor*, but conduction is not one of the five P3 properties. Heat conductors come at **P4** Heat, where the good conductors are metals and the poor conductors are wood, plastics, air and rubber. Electrical conductors and insulators come at **P5** Electrical System. Do not set a conduction question as P3 Diversity of materials.
- **Answer pattern for "why is X made of Y":** name the **property**, then link it to the **use**. For example: *The raincoat is made of plastic because plastic is waterproof, so water does not soak through to the wearer.* A property with no link to the use does not answer the question. For the full chain, see the five moves in [`../science-answering-techniques.md`](../science-answering-techniques.md).
- **Common confusions:**
  - An object and the material it is made of are different things. A *plastic bottle* is an object, and *plastic* is the material.
  - One object can be made of several materials, each chosen for a different property. A pencil has wood for the body and rubber for the eraser.
  - Whether an object floats can depend on its shape, not only on its material, such as a metal boat. Keep P3 answers to the observation and do not explain why.
