# Science concepts — Systems

**This is not MOE syllabus.** It is classroom revision notes for the Systems theme — the key ideas, definitions, examples, classifications, experiments and everyday applications as they are commonly taught for PSLE. It carries no authority over what a level covers: [`../science.md`](../science.md) is the authority on content and on every boundary, and wherever these notes and it disagree, it wins. Apply these notes, but do not quote them as MOE.

The router is [`../science-concepts.md`](../science-concepts.md); answer frames for open-ended questions are in [`../science-answering/living-things.md`](../science-answering/living-things.md) (plant and human systems) and [`../science-answering/energy-forces-and-electricity.md`](../science-answering/energy-forces-and-electricity.md) (electricity, electromagnets).

A **system** is made of different parts, each with its own function; the parts work together to perform a function. Every topic below is that idea applied to one system.

Terms marked *not required* are classroom vocabulary that `science.md` records as outside the syllabus. Teach the idea in the in-boundary phrasing given beside it; do not mark a child down for leaving the term out.

## Human body systems — P4

The P4 outcome is an **overview**: name each system and state its function. Detailed knowledge of the skeletal and muscular systems — names of bones or muscles, how they work — is not required.

| System | Function | Parts |
|---|---|---|
| **Digestive** | breaks down food into simpler substances so that it can be absorbed by the body | mouth, gullet, stomach, small intestine, large intestine |
| **Respiratory** | takes in oxygen needed by the body; removes carbon dioxide from the body | nose, windpipe, lungs |
| **Circulatory** | carries digested food, water and oxygen to all parts of the body; carries carbon dioxide and waste materials away from all parts of the body | heart, blood, blood vessels |
| **Skeletal** | supports the body and gives it shape; protects the delicate organs | — |
| **Muscular** | works with the skeletal system to allow parts of the body to move | — |

- Classroom examples of protection — the skull protects the brain, the ribcage protects the heart and lungs — go beyond the overview (*names of bones* — not required; say *the skeleton protects delicate organs such as the brain, heart and lungs*).
- The respiratory and circulatory parts are developed at P5 (below); at P4 the system names and one-line functions are enough.

## Human digestive system — P4

**Digestion** is breaking food down into **simpler substances** that can be absorbed into the blood. The path food takes, in order:

| Part | What happens | Digestion here? |
|---|---|---|
| **Mouth** | Teeth chew and grind food into smaller pieces, increasing the exposed surface area for digestive juices to act on, so digestion is faster. Saliva contains digestive juice and softens and moistens food so it is easier to swallow. The tongue rolls food into balls for swallowing. | **Yes — digestion begins here** |
| **Gullet** | Muscles in its wall push food down into the stomach. | **No** |
| **Stomach** | Contains digestive juices; churns food and mixes it with them, breaking it down into simpler substances. | Yes |
| **Small intestine** | Contains digestive juices; **most digestion** takes place here and digestion is completed here. Digested food is **absorbed into the bloodstream** through its wall. | Yes — and absorption of digested food |
| **Large intestine** | **Absorbs water** (and mineral salts) from the undigested food. | **No** |

- Undigested waste leaves the body through the **anus** (*the anus is not one of the five required parts*; it can be named, but a question will not depend on it).
- **If the large intestine is not working well**, water is not absorbed from the undigested food, so faeces become watery.
- The inner wall of the small intestine has many tiny folds (*villi* — not required) that increase the surface area for faster absorption of digested food into the blood. Say *the inner wall has many folds, which give a larger surface area*.
- **Common confusions:** the gullet does not digest; the large intestine absorbs water, not digested food; *breaking up* (chewing, churning — smaller pieces) is not *breaking down* (digestive juices — simpler substances). The frame for this distinction is in `living-things.md`.
- This topic has no Practices column entries in `science.md`, so there is no required experiment to set here.

## Plant parts and functions — P4

The required parts at P4 are **leaf, stem and root**. Flowers and fruits appear in classroom notes here but their functions belong to P5 reproduction; flowering and non-flowering plants as groups belong to P3 Diversity.

| Part | Functions |
|---|---|
| **Leaf** | Traps light and makes food for the plant (photosynthesis — the P6 topic). Takes in and gives out gases through tiny openings, mostly on the underside (*stomata* — not required; say *tiny openings on the leaf*). Water is lost from the leaves as water vapour. |
| **Stem** | Holds the plant upright so the leaves receive as much light as possible; supports branches, leaves, flowers and fruits. Carries **water** (and mineral salts) from the roots to other parts, and **food** from the leaves to other parts. |
| **Root** | Anchors the plant firmly in the ground so it is not easily uprooted or blown away. Absorbs water and mineral salts from the soil. Tiny root hairs increase the surface area so that more water is absorbed. |

- At P4 say only that the stem *transports food and water*. The **food-carrying and water-carrying tubes** are introduced at P5 — that is a `science.md` boundary.
- Water lost from the leaves as vapour is often called *transpiration* in class. The term is not on the syllabus list; *water loss through the leaves* says the same thing. *Transpiration pull* is explicitly not required.
- Classroom extras for flowers and fruits: once a plant flowers it is treated as an adult plant ready to reproduce; fruits are found only in flowering plants and protect the seeds inside.
- **Flowering vs non-flowering plants** (P3 Diversity, as a reminder): flowering plants reproduce by seeds; non-flowering plants bear no flowers, fruits or seeds and reproduce by **spores** (ferns such as bird's nest fern, staghorn fern, maidenhair fern; moss). Classroom lists also put algae here.
- *Practices:* observe plant parts.

## Plant transport system — P5 (Standard and Foundation)

The stem, leaves and roots contain two kinds of tubes:

| Tube | Carries | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| **Water-carrying tubes** (*xylem* — not required) | water and mineral salts | from the **roots** up to all other parts of the plant |
| **Food-carrying tubes** (*phloem* — not required) | food made in the leaves | from the **green leaves** to all other parts of the plant, including down to the roots |

- Use only the descriptive names. Recall of the **relative positions** of the two kinds of tube (in cross-sections of leaf, stem and root) is not required, so do not set or teach the cross-section diagrams for labelling.
- **Standard** *describes* the parts and their functions and *investigates* transport; **Foundation** *recognises* that water goes from roots to other parts and food from leaves to other parts, and *observes* transport.

**The ring experiment** (the standard transport set-up). The outer ring of a stem is removed all the way round, which removes the food-carrying tubes but leaves the water-carrying tubes intact.

| Observation | Explanation |
|---|---|
| After about a week, the stem **swells just above the ring**. | Food made by the leaves is carried downwards in the food-carrying tubes; at the ring there are no food-carrying tubes, so food cannot pass and accumulates above the cut. |
| With two rings on one stem, a fruit between two rings with **more leaves** around it grows **larger** than a fruit with fewer leaves. | More leaves make more food, and that food is carried to and stored in the nearer fruit. |

## Human respiratory system — P5 (Standard and Foundation)

**Air** is made up of gases such as **nitrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide and water vapour**. Classroom figures: about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, 0.03% carbon dioxide, the rest water vapour and other gases. The percentages are not a syllabus outcome; the list of gases is.

Required parts: **nose, windpipe, lungs** — Standard *describes* their functions, Foundation *states* them.

| Part | Function |
|---|---|
| **Nose** | Air enters and leaves the body. Hairs trap dust from inhaled air; mucus warms and moistens it. |
| **Windpipe** | Carries air from the nose towards the lungs. |
| **Lungs** | Where **gaseous exchange** takes place: oxygen from the air passes into the blood, and carbon dioxide passes from the blood into the air to be breathed out. |

Pathway of air in: **nose → windpipe → lungs**.

Classroom notes go further. All of the following is more detail than the syllabus requires (*detailed knowledge of the respiratory system, e.g. alveoli, is not required*); recognise it if a child brings it, but do not set or mark on it:

- Inside the lungs the windpipe branches into **air tubes** ending in tiny **air sacs** (the *alveoli*), lined with tiny blood vessels (*capillaries* — not required; say *tiny blood vessels* or *blood vessels*). The many air sacs give a large surface area for faster gaseous exchange. In-boundary: *the lungs have a large surface area for oxygen to pass into the blood*.
- **Breathing in and out**: breathing in, a sheet of muscle below the lungs (the diaphragm) contracts and flattens, the ribcage moves up and out, the chest space gets bigger and air is drawn into the lungs; breathing out is the reverse — diaphragm relaxes and curves up, ribcage moves down and in, chest space gets smaller and air is forced out. The **lung model** (a bell jar for the ribcage, a straw for the windpipe, a Y-shaped tube for the air tubes, two balloons for the lungs, a rubber sheet for the diaphragm) demonstrates this: pulling the sheet down inflates the balloons.

**Inhaled vs exhaled air** — within scope, because it follows directly from what the lungs do:

| Compared with inhaled air, exhaled air has | Why |
|---|---|
| **less oxygen** | oxygen is taken into the blood in the lungs |
| **more carbon dioxide** | carbon dioxide from respiration in body cells is given out in the lungs |
| **more water vapour**, and it is **warmer** | water vapour and heat are given off from the body |
| the **same amount of nitrogen** | nitrogen is not used by the body |

A common confusion: exhaled air still contains oxygen — less than inhaled air, not none.

**Smoking** (a classroom application): tar and other residue from cigarette smoke coat the inside of the lungs, reducing the surface in contact with inhaled air, so gaseous exchange is slower. A smoker breathes faster to obtain the same amount of oxygen for respiration and to remove the same amount of carbon dioxide.

**How other living things take in oxygen** — *Standard Practices* compare plants, fish and humans; *Foundation* compares plants and humans only:

| Organism | Organ for gaseous exchange | Oxygen taken from |
|---|---|---|
| Human | lungs | the air |
| Fish | **gills** | oxygen **dissolved in the water** — water enters through the mouth and passes over the gills; oxygen is absorbed into blood vessels in the gills and carried round the body; carbon dioxide is given out through the gills |
| Plant | leaves (through tiny openings) | the air |

## Human circulatory system — P5 (Standard and Foundation)

Required parts: **heart, blood, blood vessels** — Standard *describes* their functions, Foundation *states* them.

| Part | Function |
|---|---|
| **Heart** | A muscular organ that pumps blood all the time to all parts of the body. |
| **Blood** | The medium of transport: carries oxygen, digested food and water to all parts of the body, and carries carbon dioxide and waste materials away to be removed. |
| **Blood vessels** | Tubes that carry blood to and from all parts of the body. Blood always travels inside them. |

**The circuit** — blood from the body, rich in carbon dioxide, goes to the heart and is pumped to the lungs; there it takes in oxygen and gives up carbon dioxide; oxygen-rich blood returns to the heart and is pumped to all other parts of the body; there oxygen and digested food pass to the body cells, and carbon dioxide and wastes pass into the blood. Body cells → heart → lungs → heart → body cells.

**Why the heart beats faster during exercise:** the body needs more energy, so the heart beats faster to supply blood rich in oxygen and digested food to the muscles more quickly, for respiration to release energy at a faster rate. A healthy person's heart rate (pulse) at rest is about 60-70 beats per minute (classroom figure). The full frame is in `living-things.md`.

Beyond what is required — recognise, do not set:

- **Heart chambers and valves** — explicitly not required. Diagrams showing the two sides of the heart (one side carrying oxygen-poor blood to the lungs, the other oxygen-rich blood to the body) are classroom detail.
- **Types of blood vessel** — *artery* (most carry oxygen-rich blood away from the heart), *vein* (most carry oxygen-poor blood back to the heart), *capillary* (tiny vessels where substances pass between blood and cells). All three terms are not required; say *blood vessels*.
- **Parts of blood** — red blood cells (carry oxygen), white blood cells (fight disease), platelets (help blood clot to heal wounds), plasma (mainly water with dissolved substances). Not in the syllabus outcomes.
- **Fish vs human circulation** — both have a heart and blood vessels carrying blood round the body (the similarity). The classroom difference, that blood passes through the human heart twice per round trip but through a fish heart once, rests on heart structure and is beyond the syllabus. The comparison `science.md` asks for is how fish take in oxygen (gills, dissolved oxygen), above.

## Systems working together — P5 (Standard only)

Recognising how the digestive, respiratory and circulatory systems integrate is a **Standard** outcome; Foundation does not have it.

- **Digestive + circulatory:** the small intestine breaks food down into simpler substances (digested food), which is absorbed into the bloodstream; the blood carries it to all parts of the body.
- **Respiratory + circulatory:** the lungs take in oxygen, which passes into the blood; the blood carries it to all body cells; carbon dioxide from the cells is carried by the blood back to the lungs to be breathed out.
- **At the cells:** digested food and oxygen are used in **respiration** to release **energy**; carbon dioxide (and water) are produced. Classroom word summary: *digested food + oxygen → energy + carbon dioxide + water*. The syllabus focus (P6) is on respiration releasing energy from food.

**Comparing transport in humans and plants** (*Standard Practices*):

| | Human circulatory system | Plant transport system |
|---|---|---|
| **Similarity** | Both have tubes that transport substances to all parts of the organism; in both, water and food are carried through tubes. | |
| Pump | needs an organ (the **heart**) to pump materials through the tubes | **no** pumping organ |
| What the tubes carry | blood carrying oxygen, digested food, carbon dioxide and water | only **food** and **water** (with mineral salts) |
| Tubes | the **same** set of tubes (blood vessels) carries everything | **separate** tubes: food-carrying tubes for food, water-carrying tubes for water |
| Route | body → heart → lungs → heart → body (a loop) | water: roots → stem → leaves and other parts; food: leaves → stem → other parts, including roots |

## Electrical system — P5 (Standard and Foundation)

An **electric circuit** made of an energy source (**battery**) and other components (**wire, bulb, switch**) forms an **electrical system**. **Electric current flows only in a closed circuit** — Standard *shows an understanding*, Foundation *states*.

**Circuit symbols** (to read and draw circuit diagrams):

| Component | Symbol in words |
|---|---|
| Battery (cell) | a long thin line and a short thick line side by side; two batteries are two such pairs in a row |
| Wire | a straight line |
| Bulb | a circle with a cross inside |
| Switch | open: a line hinged up away from the contact; closed: the line lying flat across both contacts |

**Open and closed circuits**

| Open circuit | Closed circuit |
|---|---|
| Components not properly connected — a **break or gap** in the circuit (an open switch, a loose wire, a blown bulb, an insulator in the path) | All components properly connected, forming a complete loop |
| No electric current flows; the bulb does not light | Electric current flows continuously; the bulb lights |

**Connecting a bulb:** for the bulb to light, one wire (or battery terminal) must touch the **metal tip** and the other the **metal casing**. Current then flows through the filament and the bulb lights. Both wires on the casing, or both on the tip, and the bulb stays dark. The bulb-by-bulb frame is in `energy-forces-and-electricity.md`.

**Variables affecting the current** (*Practices: construct circuits from diagrams; investigate*):

| Change | Effect on current | Effect on bulb(s) | Stream |
|---|---|---|---|
| More **batteries in series** | more current through the bulb | **brighter** | both |
| More **bulbs in series** | less current through each bulb | each **dimmer**; if one blows, the circuit is open and **all go out** | both |
| More **bulbs in parallel** | current through each bulb stays the same | each as bright as a single bulb; if one blows, the others **stay lit** because current still flows through the other paths | **Standard only** |

- **Series** = one path for the current; **parallel** = more than one path.
- **Application:** homes are wired in parallel, so if one light blows, current still reaches the other lights and appliances.

**Electrical conductors and insulators** (both streams identify them)

| Electrical conductors | Electrical insulators |
|---|---|
| Allow electricity to flow through | Do not allow electricity to flow through |
| **All metals** are conductors; good electrical conductors are usually good conductors of heat too | **Most non-metals** are insulators |
| Examples: copper, silver, mercury, graphite, water, salt solution | Examples: glass, plastic, rubber, paper, wood, cloth, clay |

**Using electricity safely** (the Attitudes outcome — proper use and handling of electricity). Electricity can cause fires and electric shocks:

- never touch switches with wet hands;
- do not connect too many appliances to one socket;
- make sure wires are not exposed (worn insulation);
- do not repair electrical equipment yourself;
- use circuit breakers and fuses.

Conserving electricity is part of the same Attitudes outcome.

## Electromagnets — P3, revisited with circuits at P5

`science.md` places this under P3 Magnets: *make a magnet by the stroke method and the **electrical method***. The electrical method is the electromagnet, and it reappears in P5 circuit questions.

- An **electromagnet** is a coil of wire wound round a rod of **magnetic material** (iron or steel). When electric current flows through the coil, the rod is **magnetised** and becomes an electromagnet.
- It is a **temporary magnet**: when the current is switched off, it loses its magnetism.
- The core must be a magnetic material. Current flows in the coil, not through the rod. A core of a non-magnetic material (glass, wood, plastic) does not become a magnet even though current flows — and the bulb in the circuit still lights. Keep magnetic materials to **iron and steel**: recall of nickel and cobalt is not required at P3.
- Frames and the glass-core misconception are in `energy-forces-and-electricity.md` and `../science-answering/misconceptions.md`.

## Beyond the current syllabus

Commonly taught for PSLE in older classroom material, and not an outcome in `science.md`. Use for enrichment only; never set it as level content or mark a child down for not knowing it.

### Cells

`science.md` has one cell outcome: P5 **Standard** reproduction, *recognise that a cell is a basic unit of life* (not at Foundation). The parts of a cell below are not in the current syllabus.

| Part | Animal cell | Plant cell | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cell membrane** | yes | yes | allows certain substances to move in and out of the cell |
| **Nucleus** | yes | yes | controls the activities of the cell; contains hereditary information |
| **Cytoplasm** | yes | yes | jelly-like substance in which cell parts are held and move |
| **Cell wall** | no | yes | gives plant cells a regular shape; every plant cell has one, so it is how to tell a plant cell |
| **Chloroplast** | no | most (not root cells or onion skin cells) | contains chlorophyll, which absorbs light energy for the plant to make food |
| **Vacuole** | no | yes | contains cell sap, which stores nutrients and mineral salts and keeps the cell firm |

### Electromagnet strength and devices

The P5 investigation in `science.md` is of batteries and bulbs on the **current**; the strength of an electromagnet is not a named outcome. Classroom material commonly adds:

- **Factors affecting strength** — more current (more batteries in series) → stronger electromagnet; more coils of wire round the rod → stronger electromagnet. Typical set-ups: two coils with different numbers of batteries, an iron nail hanging on a string swings towards the stronger one; two coils with different numbers of turns, the one with more turns picks up more paper clips. Variables kept the same: the rod, the other factor, the paper clips.
- **Electric bell** — pressing the switch closes the circuit; the electromagnets attract an iron bar whose hammer strikes the bell; as the bar moves, it leaves a contact point, opening the circuit; the electromagnets lose their magnetism and a spring returns the bar; the contact closes again, and the repetition rings the bell until the switch is released.
- **Circuit breaker** — an automatic switch that protects a circuit from too much current. At normal current the electromagnet is not strong enough to pull the iron bar; when too much current flows the electromagnet becomes stronger, attracts the bar and separates the contacts, opening the circuit and cutting off electricity; a reset button and spring close the contacts again.
