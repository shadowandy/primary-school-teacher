# Science frames — heat, evaporation and matter

Themes: Energy (heat), Cycles (matter and water). Heat and evaporation sit together because a question about water lost from a steel beaker needs the chain to run through both.

The five moves every answer makes, the boundary notes and the pitch are in [`../science-answering-techniques.md`](../science-answering-techniques.md); the misconceptions to name when marking are in [`misconceptions.md`](misconceptions.md).

## Heat and temperature

- **Heat flow** — *The cooler Object 1 gains heat from the warmer Object 2* / *the warmer Object 1 loses heat to the cooler Object 2*. Both objects named, each tagged warmer or cooler.
- **Rate, via the conductor** — *X is a poor conductor of heat and gains heat from / loses heat to Y slowly* → the temperature of X changes slowly. Swap in *good* and *quickly* for the other direction.
- **A conductor between two things** — *Object P is a poor conductor of heat. P conducts heat from the warmer Object 1 to the cooler Object 2 at a slower rate* → Object 1 retains more heat. This is the frame behind trapped air in fur or feathers. The good-conductor direction is the same sentence with *good* and *at a faster rate*: *Iron is a good conductor of heat. Iron conducts heat from the warmer flame to the cooler wax at a faster rate* → the wax melts at a faster rate. *Conducts heat from … to …* is the phrase being marked; dropping it leaves only "steel is a better conductor", which is a statement about the material and not about the question.
- **Expansion** — *The cooler Object 1 gains heat from the warmer Object 2. Object 1 then expands and takes up more space.* **Contraction** — *The warmer Object 1 loses heat to the cooler Object 2. Object 1 then contracts and takes up less space.* Write the full phrase, both clauses.
- **Everything the heat reaches expands, not only the named substance** — a bottle of liquid X standing in hot water, with air trapped above the liquid and a glass tube through the stopper: *Both the cooler liquid X and the air in the bottle gain heat from the hot water. Both expand and take up more space. As there is not enough space in the bottle, the air pushes liquid X downwards, so liquid X moves up the glass tube and flows out of the upper end*. Account for every substance inside the set-up, the trapped air included.
- **Which set-up holds more heat** — the greater volume of the same substance at the same temperature holds more heat energy, so *the water in set-up A holds the greater volume of water at the same temperature and holds more heat energy* → the egg in A receives the most heat and cooks in the shortest time.

Comparing two materials is a comparison of **rate of heat transfer**, never of temperature: two plates out of the same refrigerator are at the same temperature, and the hand feels colder on steel because it loses heat to steel faster.

## Evaporation and condensation

Factors, each written as a one-way implication:

- greater **exposed surface area** of the water, or of the wet fabric → faster evaporation. What is exposed is the water's top surface, not the container — the container's surface area is not a factor, and water with no exposed surface does not evaporate at all.
- higher **temperature** of the water, or receiving more heat → faster evaporation.
- presence of **wind** → faster evaporation.

- **Evaporation** — *Water gains heat from the warmer surroundings and evaporates into water vapour*.
- **Condensation** — *Warmer water vapour comes into contact with the cooler surface of X, loses heat, and condenses into water droplets*. Three clauses: contact, heat lost, change of state.

Two topics often arrive together here, and the chain has to run through both. Two beakers of the same volume of water, one steel and one glass, left in the sun, with more water lost from the steel one: *Steel is a better conductor of heat than glass. Steel conducts heat from the surroundings to the water at a faster rate than glass.* **Result:** *the water in beaker A gains heat more quickly and evaporates at a faster rate than the water in beaker B, so more water is lost*.

## Three states of matter

Hold the property table. Write *fixed*, not *definite* — fewer spelling errors.

| State | Fixed shape? | Takes the shape of its container? | Fixed volume? | Can be compressed? | Takes up the whole space of its container? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Solid | yes | no | yes | no | no |
| Liquid | no | yes | yes | no | no |
| Gas | no | yes | no | yes | yes |

- **Displacement** — *Substance A moves into the container to occupy the space. Substance B is displaced out*.
- **More space made available** — *…air contracts and takes up less space. As there is more space in the flask, the ink droplet moves to take up the space*.
- **Not enough space** — *…air expands and takes up more space. As there is not enough space, air moves out of the flask through the tube and pushes the ink droplet*.
- **Cannot move in** — *Trapped air takes up the space inside the container. There is not enough space for the water to move in* → the water drips in only very slowly.
- **Volume of air in a sealed container** — solids and liquids have a fixed volume, so the air takes whatever space they leave: capacity minus the volume of the solids and liquids. A sealed container of capacity 1000 cm³ holding 200 cm³ of water and 100 cm³ of stones holds 700 cm³ of air; pumping in a further 100 cm³ of air leaves it at 700 cm³, because *when the pump is used, the air already inside the container compresses to make space for the air entering, and all the air still takes up the same amount of space inside the container*. The available space is what is fixed, so the pumped-in air is neither added to the total nor subtracted from it. "Air has no fixed shape" is true but is the wrong property for this question; "air has no fixed volume" is half the answer without compression doing the work.
